# Sampling & beyond: The model in the loop

<Byline readingTime={metadata.readingTime} />


Here is the fact this book has been building toward since Chapter 2: **a language model never outputs text.** It outputs a probability distribution over ~100,000 tokens, every step, and something outside the model must decide what to do with it. That decision (sampling) is the last mile between the frozen artifact (the trained model) of Parts II–III and the products you use. And once you see the model as "a distribution you query in a loop," the rest of the modern stack stops being magic and becomes control flow, in four escalations:

1. **Reshape the distribution** — temperature, top-k, top-p: the knobs on every API.
2. **Constrain it** — mask the illegal tokens and malformed output becomes impossible: structured output.
3. **Feed it back to itself** — chain of thought: tokens as scratch space, compute bought with length.
4. **Make the tokens executable** — tool calls, retrieval, and finally the agent: the loop, closed.

## Temperature and friends

The distribution arrives as logits; softmax turns them into probabilities. The knobs you've seen in every API reshape that distribution before drawing from it:

**Temperature** rescales the logits before the softmax turns them into probabilities:

$$
p_i \propto e^{\tip{the logit for token i}{y_i} \,/\, \tip{the temperature knob}{T}}
$$

Two things to unpack in that line. The $\propto$ ("proportional to") is just softmax's normalization left implicit: compute $e^{y_i/T}$ for every token, then divide each by their total so they sum to 1 (Chapter 1's softmax exactly, with every logit divided by $T$ first).

The second thing is a fair objection: *doesn't dividing everything by the same number change nothing?* For two nearby operations, it genuinely does change nothing. Scale the final *probabilities* by a constant and the normalization divides it right back out. *Add* a constant to every logit and it cancels too: $e^{y_i + c} = e^c \cdot e^{y_i}$, and the $e^c$, appearing in every term, is killed by the normalization (softmax is shift-invariant; implementations exploit this, subtracting the max logit for numerical stability). But *dividing* the logits survives, because the exponential converts logit **gaps** into probability **ratios**: a gap of $g$ becomes a ratio of $e^g$, so rescaling the logits rescales every gap, and therefore reshapes every ratio. Shift-invariant, scale-*sensitive*; temperature lives in that asymmetry. Run three candidates through it, with logits *mat* 4.0, *sofa* 2.0, *floor* 1.0:

- At $T = 1$: **84% / 11% / 4%** — the model's honest beliefs.
- At $T = 0.5$ every gap doubles, so every ratio squares (mat's 7.4× edge over sofa becomes 55×): **98% / 1.8% / 0.2%**, nearly deterministic.
- At $T = 2$ every gap halves, every ratio square-roots: **63% / 23% / 14%** — the underdogs breathe.

Push $T \to 0$ and the leader takes everything (greedy decoding); push it high and the distribution flattens toward uniform. Low temperature is *not* "more accurate" — it's more *conservative*: you're trading away diversity for **mode-seeking**, statistics' term for always heading toward the *mode*, the distribution's highest peak: harvesting the single most-probable neighborhood and ignoring the model's other live hypotheses. That's the right trade for extraction tasks and often the wrong one for writing and brainstorming. (Its trained-in cousin deserves a name too: **mode collapse**. Post-training's reward optimization (Chapter 8) concentrates probability mass so aggressively that whole regions of the base model's distribution effectively vanish: ask an aligned model for a joke at *any* temperature and you'll keep meeting the same few. Temperature can only redistribute what survived training; it can't resurrect modes the reinforcement learning already pruned.)

Why not always take the argmax? Because pure greed degenerates: models fall into repetition loops ("I really really really really…") — each repetition making the next more probable. And why not sample honestly? Because of the **long tail**: the bottom 90,000 tokens each have tiny probability but *collectively* enough mass that an occasional catastrophic token gets drawn, and one bad token compounds — it enters the context and conditions everything after.

Call that compounding the **autoregressive original sin** (autoregressive, from Chapter 3: each token is generated conditioned on everything before it). Generation has no backspace: a sampled token is committed to the context permanently, and every later distribution conditions on it *as if it were ground truth*. Worse, training never prepared the model for its own mistakes: during pre-training the context was always real text (a setup called **teacher forcing**), so the model has only ever practiced continuing *good* prefixes. The first off-distribution token pushes it into territory it never trained in, where its next prediction is a little worse, which pushes it further out — errors compound with length (the textbook name is **exposure bias**). You've already seen the consequences from other chapters' angles:

- An early wrong claim gets *elaborated* rather than corrected, because downstream tokens treat it as established fact (the compounding half of hallucination; Chapter 8 covered its origin, rewarded guessing).
- The reasoning models' trained habit of saying "wait, let me recheck" (Chapter 8's RLVR) is precisely the workaround. A model that can't delete a bad token can learn to append its disavowal.

The fix, from [The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751), is to amputate the tail: **top-k** keeps the k most likely tokens; **top-p** ("nucleus") keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p — adaptively narrow when the model is confident, wide when it isn't. Modern defaults (temperature ~0.7–1.0 with top-p ~0.9–0.95) are the field's accumulated scar tissue, not theory.

Two more entries complete the API-knob tour. **Frequency and presence penalties** attack repetition directly instead of hoping truncation catches it: subtract a small amount from the logit of every token already in the context (scaled by its count, or flat), so the loop-making tokens get progressively less attractive.

And one classic needs a burial notice. Greedy decoding is *myopic*: the locally best token can lead into a dead end, while the best full *sequence* may open with the second-best token. Concretely:

- Token A has probability 0.5 but its best continuation only 0.2 (total: 0.10).
- Token B has 0.4 with a 0.9 continuation (total: 0.36).

Greedy takes A and never learns what B offered. Searching every sequence is $|V|^n$ (vocabulary size to the power of sequence length) — hopeless — so **beam search** is the bounded compromise. Carry the $B$ most probable partial sequences (the "beam," typically 4–10) and at each step extend *all* of them with each candidate next token, re-rank every continuation by total log-probability (summing logs ranks identically to multiplying the probabilities above), and keep the top $B$; when the candidates finish, return the winner. $B = 1$ is greedy; a wider beam is a wider flashlight over the sequence tree. This was the standard decoder of the machine-translation era, where there *is* a near-correct target and "most probable sequence" is exactly what you want. It's essentially never used for open-ended generation, because maximizing total sequence probability is precisely the degenerate-repetition objective: the nucleus-sampling paper's most striking figure shows beam-search output looping while human text keeps choosing "surprising" tokens.

One myth to retire: temperature 0 does not make an API deterministic. Your request gets continuously batched (Chapter 11) with strangers' traffic, batch composition changes floating-point reduction orders, and floating-point addition isn't associative. That last claim, in two lines of 32-bit floats: $(2^{24} + 1) + 1 = 2^{24}$, because adding 1 to $2^{24}$ falls below the format's resolution and rounds away — twice — while $2^{24} + (1 + 1) = 2^{24} + 2$, which *is* representable. Same three numbers, different grouping, different answer. Now scale it up: every logit is a sum over thousands of products, and the GPU splits that sum across cores differently depending on how many strangers share your batch — so the result wobbles in its last digits. Run the same prompt alone at 3 a.m. and batched at noon: a logit comes back 12.4327814 instead of 12.4327829, and if the runner-up token sat inside that sliver, the argmax flips. One flipped token enters the context, and the compounding above does the rest — two "greedy, deterministic" runs, two different answers. Bit-identical reruns are a systems guarantee, not a sampling setting.

## Structured output: sampling with a seatbelt

You want JSON matching a schema; the model wants to emit prose. The robust fix isn't prompting harder — it's **constrained decoding** (the decode-time move promised back in Chapter 8): compile your schema or grammar into a token-level automaton, and at each step *mask the logits* of every token that would violate it — set them to $-\infty$, and the softmax renormalizes over what's legal:

<ConstrainedFigure />

The model's distribution proposes; your grammar disposes. Malformed output becomes *impossible*, not just unlikely — this is what "JSON mode" and schema-enforced APIs do under the hood. (APIs also expose the raw lever as `logit_bias` — a per-token nudge or outright ban you set by hand, the manual version of the same mask.) It's the cleanest illustration of this chapter's theme: the distribution is an interface, and you're allowed to program against it. (Same interface powers observability: the **logprobs** an API returns are a per-token confidence channel — poor at **calibration** (a 70%-probability token isn't right 70% of the time), but a fine anomaly detector for retrieval failures and hallucination-prone spans.)

One honest caveat: a careless grammar is a *forced-hallucination machine*. The mask is applied token by token, blind to where the model was going: when it forbids the model's preferred token, the renormalization promotes tokens the model ranked low — and everything after must continue a prefix the model would never have written, the autoregressive original sin from above, now self-inflicted. The sharpest version is a schema that **requires a value the model doesn't have**. If the field `email` must match an email pattern and the model's honest next tokens were `null` or `"unknown"`, the mask forbids honesty — so the model fabricates a syntactically perfect address, indistinguishable from a real one. The grammar didn't just permit a hallucination; it *mandated* one. And the tax is measurable beyond corner cases: [Let Me Speak Freely?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442) found reasoning accuracy declining as output-format constraints tighten.

The craft follows from the mechanism: give schemas escape hatches (nullable fields, an `"other"` enum option, somewhere to express uncertainty), and let the model finish *thinking* before the mask arrives. Reason in free prose first and extract with the grammar in a second pass, or simply put a scratchpad field first in the schema so the constrained part comes last. Constrain the envelope, not the prose inside it.

## Chain of thought: buying compute with tokens

[Chain-of-thought prompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903) began as a curiosity — ask the model to "think step by step" and accuracy on math jumps. Chapter 3 explains why it *works*: a transformer spends a fixed forward pass per token, so the only way to get more computation on a hard problem is **more tokens**. The chain of thought is scratch space — intermediate state serialized into the context, where attention can retrieve it.

The idea has climbed a long ladder in four years, and you've seen every rung. It began as a *prompt trick* (2022): literally appending "think step by step" to the prompt made accuracy jump, no training involved. Then post-training absorbed it (Chapter 8): models trained on verifiable rewards (RLVR) *discovered* extended reasoning on their own (double-checking, backtracking), and thinking-before-answering became a trained-in behavior you no longer have to ask for. By 2025 it had become a *product with a price tag*: reasoning models bill for their thinking tokens, which means you now buy accuracy with inference compute directly — this is the "third scaling axis" Chapter 4 promised when model size and data ran out of road. One caution to carry over from Chapter 6: interpretability research has caught models whose written reasoning diverges from the mechanism that actually produced the answer, so read the transcript as scratch work that helped produce the answer, and as evidence rather than an audit log.

The cheapest upgrade on top of it is **self-consistency**: sample several chains of thought at some temperature and take the *majority* final answer. Concretely: give a math word problem to the model five times at temperature 0.7. Three chains take different routes (one computes the unit price first, another scales up a ratio) and all land on **$12**; one makes an arithmetic slip and says $10; one misreads the question and says $18. Vote: $12. The trick works because correct reasoning is an *attractor*: right answers coincide, while mistakes scatter (the $10 and the $18 don't reinforce each other). You're climbing Chapter 9's pass@k curve (the odds that at least one of $k$ samples is right) without the verifier that normally checks which one; a tally suffices. The [original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171) lifted grade-school-math accuracy by ~18 points with nothing but 40 samples and a vote. The one requirement is a short, extractable final answer to tally, like a number or a multiple-choice letter; no two chains produce the same *essay*, so for open-ended output the family's heavier members take over — sample several, have a model *judge* the best.

## Tool use: tokens as system calls

The model can't browse, compute, or query your database. It can only emit tokens — so the stack made tokens *executable*. **Function calling**: describe your APIs in the prompt. The model (post-trained specifically for this — Chapter 8's fingerprints) emits a structured call like `{"name": "get_weather", "arguments": {"city": "Paris"}}`. **Your code**, not the model, executes it. The result is appended to the context. Generation resumes, now conditioned on real data.

Squint and it's a syscall interface: an untrusted process (the model) requests privileged operations from a runtime (your code) that mediates everything. The model never *does* anything — it writes requests, and the blast radius is exactly what your runtime chooses to honor.

## Retrieval: the standard answer to frozen weights

**Retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG), the most deployed pattern of the era, is the tool loop above with a search tool. The pipeline, end to end: split your documents into chunks and embed each one as a vector, offline; at query time, embed the question, fetch the nearest chunks, and paste them into the context, so the model answers from retrieved text rather than frozen memory. Of everything in this book, this is the thing you're most likely to build:

<RagFigure />

Two design decisions follow from the mechanism. **Chunking** is a real choice: a few hundred tokens per chunk — large enough to carry meaning, small enough that several winners fit in the context (and every retrieved token is one you pay prefill — Chapter 11's read-the-prompt-in compute — for). And the **reranker** exists because embedding search is blind to the pairing: each chunk's vector was computed *before anyone knew the question*, so a second model that reads query and chunk *together* catches matches that geometry alone misses — a precision pass stacked on a recall pass. (The jargon, if you shop for these: the embedding model is a *bi-encoder*, the reranker a *cross-encoder*.)

Fine-tune for behavior, retrieve for knowledge (Chapter 8's advice, now with its mechanism). Retrieval is also the standard answer to the **knowledge cutoff**: the weights froze when pre-training ended, and everything the world has done since reaches the model only through its context.

## Agents: the loop, closed

Put everything in this chapter in a `while` loop and you have an **agent**: model proposes an action ([ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) established the reason-act-observe cadence; [Toolformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761) showed models could learn tool use), runtime executes, observation enters the context, repeat until done:

<AgentLoopFigure />

That's the whole trick — the coding agents and computer-using assistants of 2026 are this loop plus enormous post-training investment in not derailing over long horizons.

The engineering discipline that emerged around it is **context engineering** — the recognition that the context window is the agent's *entire working memory*, and curating it (what to retrieve and summarize, what to evict; note how agent transcripts hit Chapter 11's prefix cache, reusing the computation for the repeated transcript prefix) is the systems half of agent quality.

The security discipline it demands is less mature. **Prompt injection** is this book's one alarm bell: instructions and data share one channel, the context, so any text an agent reads (a webpage, an email) can *try to reprogram it*, and an agent with tools turns that into real-world side effects. (Keep it distinct from **jailbreaking** — a *user* talking the model past its own refusal training. A jailbreak attacks the model's character, and the user is the attacker. Injection attacks *your application* through content it reads, with your user as the victim — and unlike jailbreaking, it gets worse the more useful you make the agent.) There is no reliable fix yet; Simon Willison's [running series](https://simonwillison.net/series/prompt-injection/) is the essential literature. Design like it's the '90s web:

- Least privilege.
- Human confirmation on irreversible actions.
- Never let untrusted input and powerful tools share a context without a plan.

That's the stack, complete — from pixels of handwritten digits to agents running your shell, every layer built from parts you now understand. What remains is to step back: what it all adds up to, what's conspicuously still broken, and where the people who build these things are pointing next. A short epilogue on where this leaves you.
