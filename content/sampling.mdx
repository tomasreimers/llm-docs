# Sampling & beyond: The model in the loop


Here is the fact this book has been building toward since Chapter 2: **a language model never outputs text.** It outputs a probability distribution over ~100,000 tokens, every step, and something outside the model must decide what to do with it. That decision — sampling — is the last mile between the frozen artifact of Parts II–III and the products you use. And once you see the model as "a distribution you query in a loop," the rest of the modern stack stops being magic and becomes control flow, in four escalations:

1. **Reshape the distribution** — temperature, top-k, top-p: the knobs on every API.
2. **Constrain it** — mask the illegal tokens and malformed output becomes impossible: structured output.
3. **Feed it back to itself** — chain of thought: tokens as scratch space, compute bought with length.
4. **Make the tokens executable** — tool calls, retrieval, and finally the agent: the loop, closed.

## Temperature and friends

The distribution arrives as logits; softmax turns them into probabilities. The knobs you've seen in every API reshape that distribution before drawing from it:

**Temperature** rescales the logits before the softmax turns them into probabilities:

$$
p_i \propto e^{\tip{the logit for token i}{y_i} \,/\, \tip{the temperature knob}{T}}
$$

Two things to unpack in that line. The $\propto$ ("proportional to") is just softmax's normalization left implicit: compute $e^{y_i/T}$ for every token, then divide each by their total so they sum to 1 — Chapter 1's softmax exactly, with every logit divided by $T$ first. The second thing is a fair objection: *doesn't dividing everything by the same number change nothing?* For two nearby operations, it genuinely does change nothing. Scale the final *probabilities* by a constant and the normalization divides it right back out. *Add* a constant to every logit and it cancels too: $e^{y_i + c} = e^c \cdot e^{y_i}$, and the $e^c$, appearing in every term, is killed by the normalization (softmax is shift-invariant — implementations exploit this, subtracting the max logit for numerical stability). But *dividing* the logits survives, because the exponential converts logit **gaps** into probability **ratios**: a gap of $g$ becomes a ratio of $e^g$, so rescaling the logits rescales every gap — and therefore reshapes every ratio. Shift-invariant, scale-*sensitive*; temperature lives in that asymmetry. Run three candidates through it, with logits *mat* 4.0, *sofa* 2.0, *floor* 1.0. At $T = 1$: **84% / 11% / 4%** — the model's honest beliefs. At $T = 0.5$ every gap doubles, so every ratio squares (mat's 7.4× edge over sofa becomes 55×): **98% / 1.8% / 0.2%**, nearly deterministic. At $T = 2$ every gap halves, every ratio square-roots: **63% / 23% / 14%** — the underdogs breathe. Push $T \to 0$ and the leader takes everything (greedy decoding); push it high and the distribution flattens toward uniform. Low temperature is *not* "more accurate" — it's more *conservative*: you're trading away diversity for **mode-seeking**, statistics' term for always heading toward the *mode*, the distribution's highest peak — harvesting the single most-probable neighborhood and ignoring the model's other live hypotheses. That's the right trade for extraction tasks and often the wrong one for writing and brainstorming. (Its trained-in cousin deserves a name too: **mode collapse**. Post-training's reward optimization (Chapter 8) concentrates probability mass so aggressively that whole regions of the base model's distribution effectively vanish — ask an aligned model for a joke at *any* temperature and you'll keep meeting the same few. Temperature can only redistribute what survived training; it can't resurrect modes the RL already pruned.)

Why not always take the argmax? Because pure greed degenerates: models fall into repetition loops ("I really really really really…") — each repetition making the next more probable. And why not sample honestly? Because of the **long tail**: the bottom 90,000 tokens each have tiny probability but *collectively* enough mass that an occasional catastrophic token gets drawn, and one bad token compounds — it enters the context and conditions everything after (the autoregressive original sin). The fix, from [The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751), is to amputate the tail: **top-k** keeps the k most likely tokens; **top-p** ("nucleus") keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p — adaptively narrow when the model is confident, wide when it isn't. Modern defaults — temperature ~0.7–1.0 with top-p ~0.9–0.95 — are the field's accumulated scar tissue, not theory.

Two more entries complete the API-knob tour. **Frequency and presence penalties** attack repetition directly instead of hoping truncation catches it: subtract a small amount from the logit of every token already in the context (scaled by its count, or flat), so the loop-making tokens get progressively less attractive. And one classic deserves a burial notice: **beam search** — keep the $B$ most probable partial sequences and extend them all, the standard decoder of the machine-translation era — is essentially never used for open-ended generation, because maximizing total sequence probability is precisely the degenerate-repetition objective; the nucleus-sampling paper's most striking figure shows beam search output looping while human text keeps choosing "surprising" tokens.

One myth to retire: temperature 0 does not make an API deterministic. Your request gets continuously batched (Chapter 11) with strangers' traffic, batch composition changes floating-point reduction orders, and floating-point addition isn't associative. Bit-identical reruns are a systems guarantee, not a sampling setting.

## Structured output: sampling with a seatbelt

You want JSON matching a schema; the model wants to emit prose. The robust fix isn't prompting harder — it's **constrained decoding** (the decode-time move promised back in Chapter 8): compile your schema or grammar into a token-level automaton, and at each step *mask the logits* of every token that would violate it — set them to $-\infty$, and the softmax renormalizes over what's legal:

<ConstrainedFigure />

The model's distribution proposes; your grammar disposes. Malformed output becomes *impossible*, not just unlikely — this is what "JSON mode" and schema-enforced APIs do under the hood. (APIs also expose the raw lever as `logit_bias` — a per-token nudge or outright ban you set by hand, the manual version of the same mask.) (One honest caveat: a mask can force the model off its preferred phrasing mid-thought, and heavy-handed grammars occasionally buy you valid JSON containing a worse answer — constrain the envelope, not the prose inside it.) It's the cleanest illustration of this chapter's theme: the distribution is an interface, and you're allowed to program against it. (Same interface powers observability: the **logprobs** an API returns are a per-token confidence channel — a poor calibration oracle, but a fine anomaly detector for retrieval failures and hallucination-prone spans.)

## Chain of thought: buying compute with tokens

[Chain-of-thought prompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903) began as a curiosity — ask the model to "think step by step" and accuracy on math jumps. Chapter 3 explains why it *works*: a transformer spends a fixed forward pass per token, so the only way to get more computation on a hard problem is **more tokens**. The chain of thought is scratch space — intermediate state serialized into the context, where attention can retrieve it.

Follow the idea through the book's arc: a prompt trick (2022), then a trained behavior via RLVR (Chapter 8), then a *pricing model* — reasoning models bill you for thinking tokens, converting inference dollars into accuracy along Chapter 4's third scaling axis. And carry Chapter 6's caveat: the transcript is scratch work that *helped produce* the answer, not a faithful log of the mechanism.

The cheapest upgrade on top of it is **self-consistency**: sample several chains of thought at some temperature and take the *majority* final answer. Different chains make different mistakes, and the mistakes rarely agree — you're climbing Chapter 9's pass@k curve without needing a verifier, just a vote. It's the simplest member of the sample-and-select family that reasoning systems industrialized.

## Tool use: tokens as system calls

The model can't browse, compute, or query your database. It can only emit tokens — so the stack made tokens *executable*. **Function calling**: describe your APIs in the prompt; the model (post-trained specifically for this — Chapter 8's fingerprints) emits a structured call like `{"name": "get_weather", "arguments": {"city": "Paris"}}`; **your code** — not the model — executes it; the result is appended to the context; generation resumes, now conditioned on real data.

Squint and it's a syscall interface: an untrusted process (the model) requests privileged operations from a runtime (your code) that mediates everything. The model never *does* anything — it writes requests, and the blast radius is exactly what your runtime chooses to honor.

**Retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG), the most deployed pattern of the era, is this loop with a search tool, and its pipeline is worth knowing because you may well build one. Offline: split your documents into **chunks** (a few hundred tokens each — large enough to be meaningful, small enough that several fit in a context), run each through an **embedding model** (Chapter 3's encoder lineage, still on the job) to get one vector per chunk, and store the vectors in an index. At query time: embed the question, fetch the nearest chunks by dot product — Chapter 2's "semantically similar means geometrically close," now a product feature — optionally **rerank** the candidates with a stronger model that reads query and chunk together, and paste the winners into the context. Fine-tune for behavior, retrieve for knowledge (Chapter 8's advice, now with its mechanism). Retrieval is also the standard answer to the **knowledge cutoff**: the weights froze when pre-training ended, and everything the world did since only reaches the model through its context.

## Agents: the loop, closed

Put everything in this chapter in a `while` loop and you have an **agent**: model proposes an action ([ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) established the reason-act-observe cadence; [Toolformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761) showed models could learn tool use), runtime executes, observation enters the context, repeat until done:

That's the whole trick — the coding agents and computer-using assistants of 2026 are this loop plus enormous post-training investment in not derailing over long horizons.

The engineering discipline that emerged around it is **context engineering** — the recognition that the context window is the agent's *entire working memory*, and curating it (what to retrieve, summarize, evict; note how agent transcripts hit Chapter 11's prefix cache) is the systems half of agent quality. The security discipline it demands is less mature, and you should hear it as this book's one alarm bell: **prompt injection**. Instructions and data share one channel — the context — so any text an agent reads (a webpage, an email, a README) can *try to reprogram it*, and an agent with tools turns that into real-world side effects. (Keep it distinct from **jailbreaking** — a *user* talking the model past its own refusal training. A jailbreak attacks the model's character, and the user is the attacker; injection attacks *your application* through content it reads, with your user as the victim — and unlike jailbreaking, it gets worse the more useful you make the agent.) There is no reliable fix yet; Simon Willison's [running series](https://simonwillison.net/series/prompt-injection/) is the essential literature. Design like it's the '90s web: least privilege, human confirmation on irreversible actions, and never let untrusted input and powerful tools share a context without a plan.

## Where this leaves you

The stack, assembled one last time, bottom to top: gradient descent tunes matrices (1) that predict tokens (2) through attention and MLPs (3), made capable by scale (4), efficient by architecture (5), partially legible by interpretability (6); trained on the internet (7), shaped into a character (8), measured imperfectly (9); run on memory-bound silicon (10), served through borrowed systems ideas (11), and finally — this chapter — wrapped in a loop that samples, constrains, and acts.

Two closing convictions, honestly held. First: the bitter lesson keeps winning, so hold your clever scaffolding loosely — a surprising amount of 2024's agent engineering became 2026's post-training, absorbed into the weights (test-time compute, better RL, longer horizons are the current frontier, and they will eat some of this chapter too). Second: everything in this book was built by people reasoning from a handful of primitives you now have — prediction, gradients, attention, scale, and a loop. The field is young, the papers are public, and the distance from "read the primer" to "contribute" has never been shorter. Go build something.
