# Sampling: Programming the distribution

<Byline readingTime={metadata.readingTime} />


Chapter 3 ended one token short of a demo. **A language model never outputs text.** It outputs a probability distribution over ~100,000 tokens, every step, and something outside the weights must decide what to do with it. That decision (sampling) is where the mathematics you just built meets the behavior you've met in every product — and once you see the model as "a distribution you query in a loop," a surprising amount of the modern stack stops being magic and becomes control flow:

1. **Reshape the distribution** — temperature, top-k, top-p: the knobs on every API.
2. **Constrain it** — mask the illegal tokens and malformed output becomes impossible: structured output.

(Making the tokens *executable* — tools, retrieval, and the agent loop — is Chapter 13, which closes the book.)

## Temperature and friends

The distribution arrives as logits; softmax turns them into probabilities. Then the two basic moves: take the single most likely token every time (**greedy decoding**: safe, prone to ruts), or *draw* a token at random in proportion to its probability (**sampling**: livelier, occasionally derailed). Every knob below navigates between those poles, reshaping the distribution before the draw:

**Temperature** rescales the logits before the softmax turns them into probabilities:

$$
p_i \propto e^{\tip{the logit for token i}{y_i} \,/\, \tip{the temperature knob}{T}}
$$

Two things to unpack in that line. The $\propto$ ("proportional to") is just softmax's normalization left implicit: compute $e^{y_i/T}$ for every token, then divide each by their total so they sum to 1 (Chapter 1's softmax exactly, with every logit divided by $T$ first).

The second thing is a fair objection: *doesn't dividing everything by the same number change nothing?* For two nearby operations, it genuinely does change nothing. Scale the final *probabilities* by a constant and the normalization divides it right back out. *Add* a constant to every logit and it cancels too: $e^{y_i + c} = e^c \cdot e^{y_i}$, and the $e^c$, appearing in every term, is killed by the normalization (softmax is shift-invariant; implementations exploit this, subtracting the max logit for numerical stability). But *dividing* the logits survives, because the exponential converts logit **gaps** into probability **ratios**: a gap of $g$ becomes a ratio of $e^g$, so rescaling the logits rescales every gap, and therefore reshapes every ratio. Shift-invariant, scale-*sensitive*; temperature lives in that asymmetry. Run three candidates through it, with logits *mat* 4.0, *sofa* 2.0, *floor* 1.0:

- At $T = 1$: **84% / 11% / 4%** — the model's honest beliefs.
- At $T = 0.5$ every gap doubles, so every ratio squares (mat's 7.4× edge over sofa becomes 55×): **98% / 1.8% / 0.2%**, nearly deterministic.
- At $T = 2$ every gap halves, every ratio square-roots: **63% / 23% / 14%** — the underdogs breathe.

Push $T \to 0$ and the leader takes everything (greedy decoding); push it high and the distribution flattens toward uniform. Low temperature is *not* "more accurate" — it's more *conservative*: you're trading away diversity for **mode-seeking**, statistics' term for always heading toward the *mode*, the distribution's highest peak: harvesting the single most-probable neighborhood and ignoring the model's other live hypotheses. That's the right trade for extraction tasks and often the wrong one for writing and brainstorming. (Training itself can also prune diversity before temperature ever gets a say — Chapter 9 names that failure when it builds the training that causes it.)

Why not always take the argmax? Because pure greed degenerates: models fall into repetition loops ("I really really really really…") — each repetition making the next more probable, because the repeated token increasingly dominates the recent context the model conditions on. And why not sample honestly? Because of the **long tail**: the bottom 90,000 tokens each have tiny probability but *collectively* enough mass that an occasional catastrophic token gets drawn, and one bad token compounds — it enters the context and conditions everything after.

Call that compounding the **autoregressive original sin** (autoregressive, from Chapter 3: each token is generated conditioned on everything before it). Generation has no backspace: a sampled token is committed to the context permanently, and every later distribution conditions on it *as if it were ground truth*. Worse, training never prepared the model for its own mistakes: during training the context was always real text from the corpus (a setup called **teacher forcing** — Chapter 2's loop never shows the model its own output), so the model has only ever practiced continuing *good* prefixes. The first off-distribution token pushes it into territory it never trained in, where its next prediction is a little worse, which pushes it further out — errors compound with length (the textbook name is **exposure bias**). You've met the consequences as a user, and later chapters return to both:

- An early wrong claim gets *elaborated* rather than corrected, because downstream tokens treat it as established fact. (When Chapter 9 dissects why models assert falsehoods at all, this is the compounding half.)
- The reasoning models you've used have a trained-in habit of saying "wait, let me recheck" — precisely this workaround. A model that can't delete a bad token can learn to append its disavowal; Chapter 9 shows the training that installs the habit.

The fix, from [The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751), is to amputate the tail: **top-k** keeps the k most likely tokens; **top-p** ("nucleus") keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p — adaptively narrow when the model is confident, wide when it isn't. Modern defaults (temperature ~0.7–1.0 with top-p ~0.9–0.95) are the field's accumulated scar tissue; nobody derived them.

The remaining API knobs, **frequency and presence penalties**, attack repetition directly instead of hoping truncation catches it, by subtracting from the logit of every token already in the context: the frequency penalty subtracts in proportion to how often the token has appeared, the presence penalty a flat amount once it has appeared at all. Either way, the loop-making tokens get progressively less attractive.

Greedy decoding is *myopic*: the locally best token can lead into a dead end, while the best full *sequence* may open with the second-best token. Concretely:

- Token A has probability 0.5 but its best continuation only 0.2 (total: 0.10).
- Token B has 0.4 with a 0.9 continuation (total: 0.36).

Greedy takes A and never learns what B offered. Searching every sequence is $|V|^n$ (vocabulary size to the power of sequence length) — hopeless — so **beam search** is the bounded compromise. Carry the $B$ most probable partial sequences (the "beam," typically 4–10) and at each step extend *all* of them with each candidate next token, re-rank every continuation by total log-probability, and keep the top $B$; when the candidates finish, return the winner. (Why *log*-probability: summing logs ranks identically to multiplying the probabilities above, and doesn't multiply hundreds of small numbers into floating-point zero.) $B = 1$ is greedy; a wider beam is a wider flashlight over the sequence tree. This was the standard decoder of the machine-translation era, where there *is* a near-correct target and "most probable sequence" is exactly what you want. It's essentially never used for open-ended generation, because maximizing total sequence probability is precisely the degenerate-repetition objective: the nucleus-sampling paper's most striking figure shows beam-search output looping while human text keeps choosing "surprising" tokens.

## Structured output: sampling with a seatbelt

You want JSON matching a schema; the model wants to emit prose. Prompting harder helps only statistically. The robust fix is **constrained decoding**: compile your schema or grammar into a token-level automaton, and at each step *mask the logits* of every token that would violate it — set them to $-\infty$, and the softmax renormalizes over what's legal:

<ConstrainedFigure />

The model's distribution proposes; your grammar disposes. Malformed output becomes *impossible*: this is what "JSON mode" and schema-enforced APIs do under the hood. (APIs also expose the raw lever as `logit_bias`, a per-token nudge or outright ban you set by hand — the manual version of the same mask.) The distribution is an interface, and you're allowed to program against it. (Same interface powers observability: the **logprobs** an API returns are a per-token confidence channel — poor at **calibration** (a 70%-probability token isn't right 70% of the time), but a fine anomaly detector: a sudden dip in per-token confidence often marks a span where the model is guessing.)

One honest caveat: a careless grammar is a *fabrication machine*. The mask is applied token by token, blind to where the model was going: when it forbids the model's preferred token, the renormalization promotes tokens the model ranked low — and everything after must continue a prefix the model would never have written, the autoregressive original sin from above, now self-inflicted. The sharpest version is a schema that **requires a value the model doesn't have**. If the field `email` must match an email pattern and the model's honest next tokens were `null` or `"unknown"`, the mask forbids honesty — so the model fabricates a syntactically perfect address, indistinguishable from a real one. The grammar didn't just permit the fabrication; it *mandated* it. (Confident fabrication has a name in this field — **hallucination** — and Chapter 9 explains why models produce it even unforced.) And the tax is measurable beyond corner cases: [Let Me Speak Freely?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442) found reasoning accuracy declining as output-format constraints tighten.

The craft follows from the mechanism: give schemas escape hatches (nullable fields, an `"other"` enum option, somewhere to express uncertainty), and let the model finish *thinking* before the mask arrives. Reason in free prose first and extract with the grammar in a second pass, or simply put a scratchpad field first in the schema so the constrained part comes last. Constrain the envelope and leave the thinking inside it free.

## What a distribution can't give you

Take stock of what these levers have in common: temperature reshapes the model's beliefs, truncation amputates their tail, a grammar forbids some of them, logprobs report them. Every move operated on *one distribution, one token at a time* — and some gaps are unreachable from there. No setting makes the model compute longer on a problem that needs it, know a fact the weights never learned, or touch anything outside its own context.

Closing those gaps is ordinary software wrapped around the loop you've had since Chapter 2: scratch space to think in, repeated samples that vote, tokens your code executes, a search index, a `while` loop. That layer — the harness — is Chapter 13, the book's last, and it can wait: every piece of it rides on a model good enough to carry it. Which leaves a strange fact sitting in plain sight. The toy transformer you can now read end to end and the model you talked to this morning differ chiefly in *size*. That can't be the whole story — can it? The next chapter is about the discovery that it nearly is: the relationship between size and ability is so lawful you can plan a frontier training run on graph paper, months before you can afford to run it.
