# Post-training: From autocomplete to assistant

<Byline readingTime={metadata.readingTime} />

GPT-3 existed for two and a half years before ChatGPT. The model that took over the world was not much more *capable* than the base model that preceded it — it was more *usable*, and the difference was post-training. If pre-training pours in the knowledge, post-training shapes the thing you actually talk to: the assistant persona, the instruction-following, the refusals, and — since 2024 — the reasoning. (The field's umbrella word for the first three — making the model's behavior match what its operators intend — is **alignment**, and you'll see it attached to half the techniques in this chapter.)

A useful frame before diving in: the base model is a simulator of documents, and the assistant is a *character* the model has been trained to play. Post-training is character design, and it runs on a startlingly small budget — for InstructGPT, the pipeline that created the ChatGPT recipe, all of post-training cost about 65 petaflop/s-days against pre-training's 3,640: **under 2% of the compute**, steering 100% of the behavior. The chapter builds the pipeline in four stages:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning** — show the model transcripts of the assistant you want. Teaches the *format*.
2. **RLHF** — grade its answers instead of writing them. Teaches *judgment*, and introduces post-training's central demon.
3. **RLVR** — replace the fallible grader with a verifier, and watch reasoning *emerge*. The 2024 turn.
4. **The practical tier** — the fine-tuning you might actually do, and what it can and can't fix.

## Supervised fine-tuning: show it the format

The first step is embarrassingly direct. Hire people (increasingly: use stronger models, with humans editing) to write ideal transcripts — conversations between a user and the assistant you *wish* existed. Then train on them with the exact same next-token loss from Chapter 1. This is **supervised fine-tuning** (SFT), and mechanically there is nothing new in it: it's more pre-training, on a tiny, curated corpus of "documents" that all happen to depict a helpful assistant. InstructGPT needed only ~13,000 such demonstrations to transform the model's behavior:

<BaseVsSftFigure />

(If you learned some ML years ago, you may know this whole pattern — train a general network once, expensively, then adapt it to tasks cheaply — as **transfer learning**. In the LLM era nobody says the term anymore, because it stopped being a technique and became the field's entire structure: the "P" in GPT stands for *pre-trained* precisely because the model is built to be adapted.)

Conversations are serialized with special tokens marking who's speaking — the **chat template**:

```
<|system|>You are a helpful assistant.<|end|>
<|user|>What's the capital of France?<|end|>
<|assistant|>Paris.<|end|>
```

(When you call a chat API, this is what your list of messages compiles to. The model is still just completing a document — the document is a screenplay, and the API stops generation when the model emits the end-of-turn token.)

What SFT can't teach well is judgment — the difference between a good answer and a great one — because writing perfect demonstrations of judgment is exactly as hard as having it.

## RLHF: grading is easier than writing

The insight that unlocked ChatGPT-level behavior, from the [InstructGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155) paper: *people are much better at comparing answers than producing them.* Ask the model "my script crashes with `KeyError: 'user_id'` — why?" and sample two answers: one dumps a corrected snippet with no explanation; the other explains that the dictionary is missing the key, shows the fix, and mentions `.get()` for safety. Few users could *write* the ideal answer — but nearly everyone picks the second, instantly. **Reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF) converts that cheap signal into training pressure.

One definition first, because this is the book's first real use of it: **reinforcement learning** (RL) is the third training paradigm. Supervised learning says "here is the correct output; imitate it." RL says "act, receive a score, and be adjusted so that high-scoring actions become more likely." No one shows the model what to do — it explores, and the score shapes it. The pipeline, in two steps:

1. **Train a reward model.** Sample pairs of model outputs, have humans pick the better one (InstructGPT used ~33,000 such comparisons), and train a separate model — typically a clone of the LLM with the token-prediction head swapped for a single score output — to predict human preference. You've now *compressed human judgment into a function* you can call millions of times an hour.
2. **Optimize against it with RL.** The LLM generates responses, the reward model scores them, and an algorithm — classically [**PPO**](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347) (proximal policy optimization) — adjusts the LLM to make high-scoring responses more probable. Unlike SFT's "reproduce this exact text," RL says "produce *whatever* scores well" — the model can discover behaviors no human demonstrated. (This is reinforcement learning's original lineage: the same family of ideas from [learning Atari and robot control from human preferences](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741), repurposed.)

<details>
<summary>What PPO actually does.</summary>

Strip the RL jargon and PPO is Chapter 1's loop with a different target. The model samples a response; the reward model hands back one score; and the question is which of the response's hundreds of token-choices to credit. PPO's answer is blunt: credit all of them, in proportion to how much *better than expected* the response scored (the **advantage** — a learned baseline predicts the expected score, so an average response produces no update at all). Then gradient-ascend: raise the log-probabilities of every token in above-expectation responses, lower them in below-expectation ones — backpropagation unchanged, just pointed at "more reward" instead of "match this text." The **proximal** part is the production-grade detail: each update is *clipped* so the new policy can't move far from the one that generated the samples — one weird batch can't yank the model somewhere strange, and the samples you paid to generate stay approximately on-policy for a few more updates. That stability knob, more than any theoretical property, is why PPO became the default: it made RL boring enough to run unattended at scale. (Note this per-step clipping is a different leash from the KL penalty below — one keeps each *update* small, the other keeps the *total drift* from the original model small.)

</details>

One guardrail matters enough to name — and to define. **KL divergence** (Kullback–Leibler) is the standard measure of how different two probability distributions are: for each token the tuned model generates, compare the probability it assigned against the probability its *frozen, pre-RL self* would have assigned, and average the log-ratios:

$$
\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) \; = \; \mathbb{E}\left[ \log \frac{\tip{probability the tuned model gave its token}{\pi(\text{token})}}{\tip{probability the frozen pre-RL model would have given it}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\text{token})}} \right]
$$

It's zero when the two models would behave identically and grows as they disagree — in Chapter 2's compression language, it's the extra bits per token you'd pay encoding the new model's output with the old model's code. RLHF subtracts this from the reward: every step of drift away from the original model now has a price, so the optimizer only moves where the reward gain is worth it. That's the **KL penalty** — a leash tethering the updated model to its pre-RL self. Without the leash, the model finds the reward model's bugs — because the reward model is a lossy proxy for what humans want, and a sufficiently strong optimizer will exploit the gap. This failure mode, **reward hacking**, is post-training's central demon: optimize "answers humans rate highly" hard enough and you get confident-sounding, agreeable, flattering answers — the well-documented **sycophancy** of chat models is widely attributed to exactly this. ("Great question!" is not politeness; it's an optimization artifact.) Goodhart's law, running at a million samples an hour.

Two important variations:

- **[Direct Preference Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)** (DPO) collapses the pipeline: a clever loss lets you train on preference pairs *directly*, no reward model, no RL loop. Far simpler and cheaper; the workhorse of open-source post-training. Frontier labs still mostly run the full RL pipeline — with online sampling and continually retrained reward models — because it can iterate past the fixed dataset that DPO is anchored to.
- **[Constitutional AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)** (Anthropic) substitutes *AI* feedback for most human feedback: a written constitution of principles, a model that critiques and revises outputs against it, and preference labels generated by the model itself (RLAIF). The scalable-oversight bet: use models to supervise models, keeping humans at the top writing principles instead of grading millions of transcripts.

<details>
<summary>The DPO loss, in one line.</summary>

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\log \sigma \Big( \beta \, \tip{how much more likely the model makes the winning answer than its reference self did}{\log \tfrac{\pi(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}} - \beta \, \tip{how much more likely it makes the losing answer}{\log \tfrac{\pi(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}} \Big)
$$

Read it as: push the winner's probability up and the loser's down — but measure both *relative to the frozen reference model*, which is the KL leash smuggled directly into the loss. The derivation's trick is showing that the optimal RLHF policy and reward model are two views of one object, so you can skip training the reward model entirely. One loss, preference pairs in, aligned model out.

</details>

## RLVR and reasoning: the 2024 turn

Everything above optimizes for *human approval* — a fuzzy, hackable signal. The breakthrough behind reasoning models was to find domains where the reward can be **verified**: the math answer is right or wrong, the code passes the tests or doesn't. **Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards** (RLVR) is RLHF with the squishy reward model replaced by a checker that cannot be flattered.

Given an unhackable reward and room to explore, something remarkable happens — documented most openly in DeepSeek's [R1 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948): the model *discovers* extended reasoning on its own. Trained only on "was the final answer right," R1's precursor spontaneously learned to produce long chains of thought, to double-check itself, to backtrack — the paper's researchers watched it learn to say "wait, let me reevaluate this step" with no one having demonstrated it:

(An algorithm note, since R1 made it famous: instead of PPO, R1 runs **GRPO** — group relative policy optimization — which exploits something verifiable rewards make cheap. PPO's accordion above needed a learned baseline to define "better than expected"; GRPO just samples a *group* of answers to the same problem, scores them all, and uses the group's average as the baseline — above your siblings, reinforced; below, suppressed. One fewer model to train, and a natural fit when scoring is as cheap as running a checker.)

<RlvrFigure />

This is the third scaling axis promised in Chapter 4 — **test-time compute** — made concrete: reasoning models convert inference tokens into accuracy, and the exchange rate is good enough that "think for 10,000 tokens" competes with "be 10× bigger." It also reframes the economics of Part IV: a reasoning model's cost lives in its inference bill, not just its training bill. (OpenAI's [o1](https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/) announced the regime; R1 showed everyone how it works.)

Two caveats to keep your model of reasoning models honest. Verifiability is a *spectrum* — math and code sit at the easy end, and extending RLVR to essay quality or medical advice requires reward models again (with all their hackability; there's active work on making the graders themselves reason). And recall Chapter 6: the chain of thought is *evidence* of the computation, not a faithful log of it.

## The practical tier: fine-tuning for the rest of us

You will probably never run RLHF, but you may well fine-tune — and the method you'd use is **[LoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)** (low-rank adaptation). The problem it solves is Chapter 7's checkpoint arithmetic in reverse: full fine-tuning needs gradients and optimizer state for every weight — ~14 bytes per parameter, >100 GB of working state for even an 8B model — which prices out single GPUs. LoRA's move: freeze every original weight, and next to each big matrix install a *bypass* of two skinny matrices — one projecting down to a tiny rank $r$, one projecting back up — so the layer computes $Wx + BAx$. Only $A$ and $B$ train:

<LoraFigure />

The bet behind it is that a fine-tune's *change* to the weights ($\Delta W = BA$, rank at most $r$) doesn't need the full matrix's expressiveness — you're nudging a mostly-right model, not relearning it — and empirically rank 8–64 captures most of what full fine-tuning gets. The economics follow: under 1% of the parameters trainable (and only *their* optimizer state in memory), a single GPU instead of a cluster, and — since the original weights never moved — adapters that can be merged into $W$ for zero-latency serving, or kept separate and hot-swapped per customer or task. (The budget variant you'll see everywhere, **QLoRA**, squeezes further: hold the frozen base weights in 4-bit — Chapter 11's quantization — and train the adapters in full precision on top, putting a 70B fine-tune within reach of one card.)

The advice half of this section: fine-tuning teaches *form* extremely well (your tone, your domain's jargon) and *facts* poorly — new knowledge injected via fine-tuning tends to be brittle and to collide with what the model already believes. For facts, retrieval (Chapter 12) usually beats weights. And for strict output *syntax* — valid JSON matching a schema — you may not need training at all: **constrained generation** masks away every token that would break the grammar at decode time, making invalid output impossible rather than unlikely (Chapter 12 covers how). Fine-tune for behavior; retrieve for knowledge; constrain for syntax. And know the tax you're paying either way: any narrow training erodes some general capability (**catastrophic forgetting**), which is why labs mix general data into every specialization run — and why a model that's been aggressively aligned can feel slightly dumber than its base (the "alignment tax," real but much reduced in modern pipelines).

## What post-training can't fix

A clean mental model to close the part on: pre-training sets the *ceiling* — the knowledge and raw capability. Post-training determines how much of the ceiling you can *reach* through the chat interface, and RLVR genuinely raises it for verifiable domains. But no amount of preference tuning installs knowledge that isn't there — and the failure users meet first deserves its mechanism spelled out.

**Hallucination** — the model confidently asserting things that aren't true — is not a bug bolted onto an otherwise truthful system; it's the default behavior of the machine this book has described. A language model never retrieves facts; it continues documents (Chapter 2), and nothing in the pre-training loss distinguishes *true* from *plausible* — a confident, well-formatted wrong answer is often literally the most likely next text. When the fact isn't in the weights, the distribution still has to put its mass somewhere, and it lands on things that *sound like* answers. Post-training then makes a bad situation worse in a specific, measurable way: graders — human raters and benchmarks alike — score a blank "I don't know" as zero and a guess as sometimes-right, so optimization teaches the model to be a confident test-taker ([OpenAI's analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664) frames hallucination as exactly this: an artifact of evaluations that reward guessing over calibrated uncertainty). The managed version is calibration — training the model to say "I don't know" when its internal confidence is genuinely low — and it's a treatment, not a cure. For facts that must be right, the engineering answer isn't a better character; it's grounding the model in retrieved sources (Chapter 12) and verifying what comes out (Chapter 9).

When a model fails you, it's worth asking which layer failed: the ceiling, or the character.
