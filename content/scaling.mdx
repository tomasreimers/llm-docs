# Scaling: Bigger is different

In 2019, Rich Sutton — one of the fathers of reinforcement learning — published a short, grumpy essay called [The Bitter Lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html). Its thesis: seventy years of AI history show that general methods that leverage more computation always, eventually, beat clever methods that encode human knowledge. Researchers hate this, because it means the craft they love loses to whoever buys more compute. They have also, repeatedly, been unable to refute it.

The transformer era is the bitter lesson's greatest vindication. The previous chapter's architecture is almost aggressively generic — identical blocks, no linguistic structure baked in. This chapter is about the discovery that turned "just make it bigger" from a hope into a *quantitative engineering discipline*, in three steps:

1. Loss falls as a **power law** in model size, data, and compute — straight lines on log-log plots that you can fit on cheap runs and extrapolate to expensive ones. Capability became predictable.
2. A fixed compute budget must be split between a bigger model and more data, and there is a measured answer — **Chinchilla's ~20 tokens per parameter** — which the industry then deliberately violates in one direction, for a good reason.
3. Loss falls smoothly, but *capabilities* arrive in lurches — **emergence** — and when the data that feeds the power laws runs short, the field finds new axes to scale.

And because the whole point of this chapter is that these laws are *used*, not admired, we'll use them. Here is the running example: **someone hands you a cluster of 1,000 GPUs for one month** and tells you to train the best model you can. By the end of the chapter you will have planned the entire run — budget, model size, dataset size, expected loss — without training anything.

## Three numbers describe a training run

Before the laws, the quantities they relate. Any training run is summarized by three numbers:

- $N$ — **parameters**: the count of weights in the model. GPT-2: 124M (we counted them at the end of Chapter 3). GPT-3: 175B.
- $D$ — **training tokens**: how much text the model sees, measured after tokenization (Chapter 2). GPT-3: ~300B tokens.
- $C$ — **compute**: the total work executed during training, counted in **FLOPs** — floating-point operations, where one FLOP is a single multiply or add of two numbers. (Careful with the units: FLOPs is a *count* of operations; a chip's speed is FLOPs *per second*, often written FLOP/s. A training run costs 10²⁴ FLOPs the way a trip costs 500 miles — how fast you cover them is the hardware's problem, and Part IV's subject.)

These aren't independent. Each training token costs a fixed amount of work proportional to the model's size, and the total is well-approximated by a rule of thumb you will see everywhere in this field:

$$
\tip{total training compute in FLOPs}{C} \approx 6 \cdot \tip{parameters}{N} \cdot \tip{training tokens}{D}
$$

<details>
<summary>Where does the 6 come from?</summary>

Count the work per token, one weight at a time. In the forward pass, a weight $w$ connecting an activation $a$ to an output does exactly one multiply and one add ($\text{out} \mathrel{+}= w \cdot a$): 2 FLOPs per weight, so a forward pass costs about $2N$ FLOPs per token. Now recall what Chapter 1's backward pass computes at that same connection — *two* things, not one. It needs the gradient with respect to the **input** ($w \cdot \partial L/\partial \text{out}$ — passing the signal backward through the multiplication, to keep the chain rule going into earlier layers), and the gradient with respect to the **weight itself** ($a \cdot \partial L/\partial \text{out}$ — the number gradient descent will actually use to update $w$). Each is its own multiply-add. Two jobs where the forward pass had one: $4N$ FLOPs. Total: $2N + 4N = 6N$ per token, times $D$ tokens. The approximation ignores attention's score computation, which is why it's an approximation — but for realistic context lengths the parameter term dominates, and everyone from research papers to GPU procurement spreadsheets uses $6ND$.

</details>

Two sanity checks with real runs. GPT-3: $6 \times 175\text{B} \times 300\text{B} \approx 3.15 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs — which matches the number in the paper. And *your* run: a modern GPU sustains on the order of $10^{15}$ FLOPs per second at peak, so 1,000 GPUs × 30 days is

$$
1{,}000 \times 10^{15} \times 2{,}592{,}000 \text{ s} \approx 2.6 \times 10^{24} \text{ FLOPs — at peak.}
$$

You will not run at peak. The fraction of theoretical FLOPs a training run actually achieves is called **MFU** (model FLOPs utilization), and a well-engineered run manages around 40% — the rest is lost to memory stalls, communication between GPUs, and everything else in Part IV. So your real budget is:

$$
C \approx 2.6 \times 10^{24} \times 0.40 \approx 10^{24} \text{ FLOPs.}
$$

A clean number. Hold onto it; the rest of the chapter spends it.

## The straight lines

Knowing your budget doesn't tell you what it buys. In 2020, [Kaplan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361) at OpenAI answered that: they trained transformers at many sizes — all small enough to be affordable — and measured how the next-token loss (Chapter 2's cross-entropy) fell as $N$, $D$, and $C$ grew. The result is arguably the most consequential set of graphs in modern computing — but it only shows up if you plot it right. The runs span a factor of a million in compute, so on ordinary axes everything cheap is crushed into the left margin; the fix is a **log-log plot**, where each tick on each axis means "×10" rather than "+10" (so the distance from 1 to 10 equals the distance from 10 to 100 — equal space per multiplication). Replotted that way, the measurements form a straight line, spanning many orders of magnitude without bending. And a straight line on log-log axes is a specific mathematical claim — it means the relationship is a **power law**:

$$
\tip{the loss you can expect at a given model size}{L(N)} \approx \left(\frac{\tip{a constant measured empirically}{N_c}}{\tip{the number of parameters}{N}}\right)^{\tip{the slope of the line — about 0.076 for parameters}{\alpha_N}}
$$

<details>
<summary>Why a straight line means a power law.</summary>

Take the log of both sides of $L = (N_c / N)^{\alpha}$:

$$
\log L \; = \; \alpha \log N_c - \alpha \log N
$$

In log coordinates that's just $y = b - \alpha x$ — a line, with the exponent $\alpha$ as its slope. The implication runs both ways: *any* power law plots straight on log-log axes, and data that stays straight across eight orders of magnitude can only be a power law. That's why the figure's slope is the law's exponent, and why "fit the line" and "measure the scaling law" are the same operation. (Contrast the other curve engineers love: an exponential $L = e^{-kN}$ plots straight on *log-linear* axes — log the y-axis only. Checking which plot straightens your data is a quick way to identify its growth regime.)

</details>

Read that the way an engineer should: **capability became predictable.** Fit the line on runs you can afford, and you know the loss of the run you can't — *before spending the money*:

<ScalingLawsFigure />

This is exactly how you de-risk your month. Before committing the cluster, you'd spend a few days training a ladder of small models — 10M, 100M, 1B parameters, each a sliver of your budget — fit the line, and read off what loss $10^{24}$ FLOPs should buy. If the eventual big run misses that number badly, something is *wrong* (a data bug, an instability), and you know it early. GPT-3 — a 175B-parameter model that cost millions to train — was not a gamble; it was an interpolation along a line OpenAI had already measured. No plateau was in sight; the line just kept going. (Gwern's [The Scaling Hypothesis](https://gwern.net/scaling-hypothesis) is the best contemporaneous account of why this was such a shock to the field's self-image.)

Two properties of these laws matter for everything downstream:

- **Power laws are brutal.** The exponents are small — $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$ for parameters, and the compute version is shallower still, around 0.05. To see what that costs, plug a 10× budget increase into the law: $L(10C) = L(C) \times 10^{-0.05} \approx 0.89 \, L(C)$. Ten times the money buys an 11% loss reduction — and because the law is a power law, the *next* 11% costs another 10×, forever. A shallow slope on a log-log plot means progress is real but priced in orders of magnitude, and that single fact explains the shape of the industry: the GPU shortages, the $100B datacenter announcements, the concentration of frontier training into a handful of labs.
- **But they are relentless.** Loss falls slowly — yet it falls *reliably*, and (as we'll see below) fixed decrements of loss keep unlocking qualitatively new behavior.

## Chinchilla: the exchange rate between size and data

Your budget is $C \approx 6ND \approx 10^{24}$, and that immediately poses a question the compute law can't answer: should you train a *bigger model* (raise $N$) or train *longer* (raise $D$)? You can't do both — the product is pinned.

Kaplan's paper offered an answer, and the answer was wrong. In 2022, DeepMind's [Chinchilla paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556) redid the measurement more carefully: train many models at *fixed* compute budgets, varying the $N$-vs-$D$ split within each budget, and see where loss bottoms out. Every budget has a sweet spot, and the sweet spots line up:

<ChinchillaFigure />

The compute-optimal rule of thumb: **about 20 tokens of training data per parameter.** The field had been training models far too large on far too little data — DeepMind's own 280B-parameter Gopher saw just 300B tokens, barely *one* token per parameter. Chinchilla, a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens (exactly the 20:1 ratio) for the same total compute, beat it across the board.

Now plan your run. The top curve in the figure *is* your budget — $10^{24}$ FLOPs — and its minimum sits at roughly $N^* \approx 95\text{B}$ parameters and $D^* \approx 1.7\text{T}$ tokens (about 18 tokens per parameter; the fitted formula's numbers, which is what the figure plots). Expected loss: about 1.96. That is the compute-optimal plan: a 95B model, 1.7 trillion tokens, one month, done. In 2022 you would have stopped here.

But compute-optimal is only the right target if **training cost is all you care about** — and every *served* model has a second budget: inference. A smaller model is cheaper on every single token it ever generates. So the industry now deliberately **over-trains**. Consider the alternative plan for your same month: train an 8B model on 15T tokens, the way Llama 3 did. That's $6 \times 8\text{B} \times 15\text{T} \approx 7 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs — it also fits your budget. The figure shows what you paid: the 8B point sits far up the left wall of its iso-FLOP curve, a visibly worse loss than the 95B plan. And it shows what you bought: a model ~12× cheaper to serve, forever. Which plan is right depends entirely on how many tokens the model will serve in its lifetime — which is why cheap, fast, surprisingly good small models exist: you are looking at this trade. ([Chinchilla's Wild Implications](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/6Fpvch8RR29qLEWNH/chinchilla-s-wild-implications) is a great meditation on the corollary: once data is the binding constraint, where does more *data* come from?)

## Emergence: when quantity reads as quality

So the laws told you your loss in advance — 1.96 for the optimal plan. What they did *not* tell you is what the model will be able to **do** at loss 1.96. Scaling laws describe the smooth decline of a training objective; users experience capabilities — and capabilities arrive in a lurchier way.

GPT-3's paper was titled [Language Models are Few-Shot Learners](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165) because of its headline surprise: show the model a few examples of a task *in its prompt*, and it performs the task — no training, no gradient updates. Nobody designed **in-context learning**; it emerged. Smaller models mostly couldn't do it; bigger ones could. Likewise multi-digit arithmetic, translation, and code generation seemed to snap from "can't" to "can" at particular scales — a phenomenon catalogued in [Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682).

You should hold this idea with some skepticism, because the sharpest version of it may be an artifact of how we grade. [Are Emergent Abilities a Mirage?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004) showed that many "discontinuous" jumps come from all-or-nothing metrics. The arithmetic makes it obvious: suppose a correct answer is 10 tokens long, and the model's per-token accuracy $p$ improves smoothly with scale. A grader that awards partial credit reports $p$ — smooth. A grader that requires the whole answer scores $p^{10}$: at $p = 0.5$ that's 0.001, at $p = 0.9$ it's 0.35, at $p = 0.98$ it's 0.82 — a cliff, manufactured entirely by the exponent:

<EmergenceFigure />

The truth seems to be: *underlying competence improves smoothly; thresholded, real-world usefulness arrives suddenly.* Both halves matter. The smooth part is why labs can forecast loss; the sudden part is why each model generation feels categorically different — and why nobody can tell you with confidence what the next 10× will unlock. For your own run it means the loss forecast is solid but the capability forecast is not: you know the model will be *better*; you don't know exactly what new thing it will be able to do.

## The data wall, and what came after

Power laws don't repeal themselves, but their inputs can run short — and your own plan shows where. Your month wanted 1.7T tokens. Now scale the plan the way the industry did: a frontier lab's budget is 100× yours, $10^{26}$ FLOPs, and the same fit asks for roughly a **trillion parameters trained on ~16 trillion tokens**. Estimates of all usable, deduplicated public text land in the low tens of trillions of tokens. The compute-optimal frontier run doesn't just want a big dataset; it wants *most of the usable internet* — and you cannot 10× a dataset that is most of the internet. By 2024 this "data wall" was reshaping the field's direction in three visible ways:

- **Data quality became the frontier.** If you can't get more tokens, get better ones — aggressive filtering, deduplication, and curriculum design (Chapter 7).
- **Synthetic data went mainstream.** Models generating, grading, and filtering training data for the next model — with the obvious snake-eating-its-tail risks carefully managed.
- **A third scaling axis opened: test-time compute.** If you can't profitably make the model much bigger, make it *think longer* — spend more tokens reasoning before answering. The reasoning models of 2024–2026 (o1, R1, and their descendants) scale capability with inference compute, and turned out to have their own scaling laws. That story belongs to post-training, and it's the climax of Chapter 8.

The meta-lesson of this chapter for an engineer: in this field, the boring, general, scalable thing wins. Keep that in mind whenever you're tempted to bet against it.
