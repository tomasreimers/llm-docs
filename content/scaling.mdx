# Scaling: Bigger is different

In 2019, Rich Sutton — one of the fathers of reinforcement learning — published a short, grumpy essay called [The Bitter Lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html). Its thesis: seventy years of AI history show that general methods that leverage more computation always, eventually, beat clever methods that encode human knowledge. Researchers hate this, because it means the craft they love loses to whoever buys more compute. They have also, repeatedly, been unable to refute it.

The transformer era is the bitter lesson's greatest vindication. The previous chapter's architecture is almost aggressively generic — identical blocks, no linguistic structure baked in. This chapter is about the discovery that turned "just make it bigger" from a hope into a *quantitative engineering discipline*, in three steps:

1. Loss falls as a **power law** in model size, data, and compute — straight lines on log-log plots that you can fit on cheap runs and extrapolate to expensive ones. Capability became predictable.
2. A fixed compute budget must be split between a bigger model and more data, and there is a measured answer — **Chinchilla's ~20 tokens per parameter** — which the industry then deliberately violates in one direction, for a good reason.
3. Loss falls smoothly, but *capabilities* arrive in lurches — **emergence** — and when the data that feeds the power laws runs short, the field finds new axes to scale.

## Three numbers describe a training run

Before the laws, the quantities they relate. Any training run is summarized by three numbers:

- $N$ — **parameters**: the count of weights in the model. GPT-2: 124M (we counted them at the end of Chapter 3). GPT-3: 175B.
- $D$ — **training tokens**: how much text the model sees, measured after tokenization (Chapter 2). GPT-3: ~300B tokens.
- $C$ — **compute**: the total number of floating-point operations (FLOPs — individual multiplies and adds) executed during training.

These aren't independent. Each token processed during training costs a fixed amount of work proportional to the model's size, and the total is well-approximated by a rule of thumb you will see everywhere in this field:

$$
\tip{total training compute in FLOPs}{C} \approx 6 \cdot \tip{parameters}{N} \cdot \tip{training tokens}{D}
$$

Run the numbers for GPT-3: $6 \times 175\text{B} \times 300\text{B} \approx 3.15 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs. To give that number texture: a single modern GPU sustains on the order of $10^{15}$ FLOPs per second, so GPT-3 is roughly ten GPU-*years* of arithmetic — a thousand GPUs for several days at perfect efficiency, weeks in practice. Frontier runs today are a thousand times larger.

<details>
<summary>Where does the 6 come from?</summary>

Count the work per token. In the forward pass, essentially every parameter participates in one multiply and one add (it sits in some matrix that the token's vector gets multiplied by), so a forward pass costs about $2N$ FLOPs per token. Backpropagation (Chapter 1) costs roughly twice the forward pass — it propagates gradients back through every multiplication *and* computes a gradient for every weight — adding $4N$. Total: $6N$ FLOPs per token, times $D$ tokens. The approximation ignores attention's score computation, which is why it's an approximation — but for realistic context lengths the parameter term dominates, and everyone from research papers to GPU procurement spreadsheets uses $6ND$.

</details>

## The straight lines

In 2020, [Kaplan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361) at OpenAI trained transformers at many sizes — all small enough to be affordable — and measured how the next-token loss (Chapter 2's cross-entropy) fell as $N$, $D$, and $C$ grew. The result is arguably the most consequential set of graphs in modern computing: plotted against any one of the three (with the other two unconstrained), loss forms a straight line on a log-log plot, spanning many orders of magnitude without bending. A straight line on log-log axes means a **power law**:

$$
\tip{the loss you can expect at a given model size}{L(N)} \approx \left(\frac{\tip{a constant measured empirically}{N_c}}{\tip{the number of parameters}{N}}\right)^{\tip{the slope of the line — about 0.076 for parameters}{\alpha_N}}
$$

Read that the way an engineer should: **capability became predictable.** Fit the line on runs you can afford, and you know the loss of the run you can't — *before spending the money*:

GPT-3 — a 175B-parameter model that cost millions to train — was not a gamble. It was an interpolation along a line OpenAI had already measured. No plateau was in sight; the line just kept going. (Gwern's [The Scaling Hypothesis](https://gwern.net/scaling-hypothesis) is the best contemporaneous account of why this was such a shock to the field's self-image.)

Two properties of these laws matter for everything downstream:

- **Power laws are brutal.** The exponents are small ($\alpha_N \approx 0.076$), which means each fixed decrement of loss costs roughly 10× more compute. This single fact explains the shape of the industry: the GPU shortages, the $100B datacenter announcements, the concentration of frontier training into a handful of labs.
- **But they are relentless.** Loss falls slowly — yet it falls *reliably*, and (as we'll see below) fixed decrements of loss keep unlocking qualitatively new behavior.

## Chinchilla: the exchange rate between size and data

The compute budget $C \approx 6ND$ immediately poses a question: given a fixed budget, should you train a *bigger model* (raise $N$) or train *longer* (raise $D$)? You can't do both — the product is pinned.

Kaplan's paper offered an answer, and the answer was wrong. In 2022, DeepMind's [Chinchilla paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556) redid the measurement more carefully: train many models at *fixed* compute budgets, varying the $N$-vs-$D$ split within each budget, and see where loss bottoms out. Every budget has a sweet spot, and the sweet spots line up:

<ChinchillaFigure />

The compute-optimal rule of thumb: **about 20 tokens of training data per parameter.** The field had been training models far too large on far too little data — DeepMind's own 280B-parameter Gopher saw just 300B tokens, barely *one* token per parameter. Chinchilla, a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens (exactly the 20:1 ratio) for the same total compute, beat it across the board.

But the more important lesson is the one the field internalized *after* Chinchilla: compute-optimal is only the right target if **training cost is all you care about**. Every *served* model has a second budget — inference — and a smaller model is cheaper on every single token it ever generates. So the industry now deliberately **over-trains**: Llama 3's 8B model trained on ~15 *trillion* tokens — 1,875 tokens per parameter, nearly 100× the Chinchilla ratio — happily paying extra training compute (visible in the figure as a worse loss than that budget could have bought) in exchange for a small model that punches above its size forever after. When you see a cheap, fast, surprisingly good small model, you are looking at this trade. ([Chinchilla's Wild Implications](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/6Fpvch8RR29qLEWNH/chinchilla-s-wild-implications) is a great meditation on the corollary: once data is the binding constraint, where does more *data* come from?)

## Emergence: when quantity reads as quality

Scaling laws describe the smooth decline of *loss*. But users don't experience loss; they experience capabilities — and capabilities arrive in a lurchier way.

GPT-3's paper was titled [Language Models are Few-Shot Learners](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165) because of its headline surprise: show the model a few examples of a task *in its prompt*, and it performs the task — no training, no gradient updates. Nobody designed **in-context learning**; it emerged. Smaller models mostly couldn't do it; bigger ones could. Likewise multi-digit arithmetic, translation, and code generation seemed to snap from "can't" to "can" at particular scales — a phenomenon catalogued in [Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682).

You should hold this idea with some skepticism, because the sharpest version of it may be an artifact of how we grade. [Are Emergent Abilities a Mirage?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004) showed that many "discontinuous" jumps come from all-or-nothing metrics. The arithmetic makes it obvious: suppose a correct answer is 10 tokens long, and the model's per-token accuracy $p$ improves smoothly with scale. A grader that awards partial credit reports $p$ — smooth. A grader that requires the whole answer scores $p^{10}$ — which sits near zero until $p$ gets large and then shoots up:

<EmergenceFigure />

The truth seems to be: *underlying competence improves smoothly; thresholded, real-world usefulness arrives suddenly.* Both halves matter. The smooth part is why labs can forecast; the sudden part is why each model generation feels categorically different — and why nobody can tell you with confidence what the next 10× will unlock.

## The data wall, and what came after

Power laws don't repeal themselves, but their inputs can run short. Chinchilla says a compute-optimal frontier run wants tens of trillions of tokens — and by 2024 the frontier labs were training on a meaningful fraction of *all usable public text*. You cannot 10× a dataset that is most of the internet. This "data wall" reshaped the field's direction in three visible ways:

- **Data quality became the frontier.** If you can't get more tokens, get better ones — aggressive filtering, deduplication, and curriculum design (Chapter 7).
- **Synthetic data went mainstream.** Models generating, grading, and filtering training data for the next model — with the obvious snake-eating-its-tail risks carefully managed.
- **A third scaling axis opened: test-time compute.** If you can't profitably make the model much bigger, make it *think longer* — spend more tokens reasoning before answering. The reasoning models of 2024–2026 (o1, R1, and their descendants) scale capability with inference compute, and turned out to have their own scaling laws. That story belongs to post-training, and it's the climax of Chapter 8.

The meta-lesson of this chapter for an engineer: in this field, the boring, general, scalable thing wins. Keep that in mind whenever you're tempted to bet against it.
