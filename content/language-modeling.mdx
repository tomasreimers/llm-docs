# Language modeling: Predicting the next token

In 1951, [Claude Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Shannon) published a paper in which he asked people to guess a sentence one letter at a time, and used their guesses to measure the statistical structure of English. Buried in that experiment is the entire premise of this book: **language has statistical structure, and predicting the next symbol is the way to capture it.**

How do we get from reading digits to a machine that writes? The answer, in three steps:

1. We pose "produce language" as a prediction problem: given text so far, guess the next token. That makes it a *classifier* — the classifier from Chapter 1, with the possible words in the vocabulary as classes.
2. The obvious approach, counting what followed what, works until it has to generalize — so words become learned *vectors*, where similar words can share what the model knows.
3. The loss is the same cross-entropy from Chapter 1 — and here it picks up a famous alter ego: a model that predicts text well *compresses* it well.

## The task: guess the next token

Play Shannon's game yourself: "the cat sat on the ___". You didn't retrieve an answer; you produced a ranking — *mat* likely, *sofa* plausible, *carburetor* absurd. A **language model** is that ranking, made precise: a function that takes the text so far and returns a probability for every possible next token:

$$
P(\tip{the next token}{x_{t+1}} \mid \tip{everything so far — the context}{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_t})
$$

Look at what this is, in Chapter 1's terms: a *classifier*. Input: the context. Output classes: every token in the vocabulary. Same logits, same softmax, same "we're doing statistics, not logic" — just ~100,000 classes instead of 10:

<NextTokenFigure />

Note what the model emits: never text, always a *distribution*. Something outside the model must decide what to do with it — take the top token, or sample from it (that decision matters enough to get Chapter 12 to itself). Generation is this classifier in a loop: predict a token, append it to the context, run again. One token at a time, no plan sitting in a buffer — any appearance of planning is implicit in the distribution at each step.

Why would such a humble objective produce systems that write code and answer questions? Because predicting the next token of *arbitrary* text, well, requires modeling whatever process produced the text. Predicting the next token of `The capital of France is` requires knowing facts. Predicting the next line of a half-finished function requires modeling the author's intent. Predicting the final chapter of a murder mystery requires having tracked every clue. Pushed to a sufficiently low loss, prediction doesn't *approximate* understanding — it demands it. (Whether it demands understanding in the same *form* humans have it is a genuinely open question; Chapter 6 is about the people prying the models open to check.)

## Before neural networks: counting

Here's the simplest language model you can build, and people built it for decades: **count**. Record, for every word, what followed it in the corpus and how often — a **bigram** model (pairs; count triples and it's a trigram, *n* words and it's an **n-gram**). Training is literally tallying. Below is a real one, trained on the eight sentences shown:

<BigramFigure />

Generate a few samples and you'll see both the magic and the ceiling. The magic: pure counting already produces locally-plausible English — this is the germ of everything in this book. The ceiling: every word knows only the single word before it, so the output wanders without meaning or memory.

The obvious fix is longer contexts — count what follows every *pair*, every *triple*, every ten-word window. Now watch it explode. With a 50,000-word vocabulary, a 10-word context has

$$
\tip{vocabulary size}{|V|}^{\,\tip{context length}{n}} = 50{,}000^{10} \approx 10^{47}
$$

possible rows in the table — more than there are atoms in the Earth. And no corpus can keep up. Be as ambitious as you like: take every page of the public internet — tens of trillions of words, the largest body of text our species has produced — and you have fewer than $10^{14}$ examples with which to fill a $10^{47}$-row table. Almost every context you'll ever encounter *has never occurred before, anywhere*. The table has no entry; the model has nothing to say.

The failure is deeper than size, and it's worth naming precisely: **counting can't share.** Having seen "the dog sat on the ___" a thousand times tells a counting model *nothing* about "the cat sat on the ___" — the contexts differ, so they're different rows, full stop. Yet any English speaker knows those contexts are nearly the same. What we need is for *similar words to have similar representations*, so that evidence about one flows to the other. Chapter 1 gave us exactly one tool for learning useful representations under pressure of a loss. We'll need it — but first we have to decide what the atoms are.

## Tokens: the atoms of an LLM

What should the unit of prediction be? Characters make sequences enormously long. Whole words can't handle typos, code, or German compound nouns — the vocabulary is effectively unbounded. Every modern LLM uses the compromise in between: **subword tokens**, discovered by an algorithm called [byte-pair encoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.07909) (BPE) that is pure Chapter-1 spirit — don't hand-design the units, learn them from data by counting. It works bottom-up. Start with the smallest possible vocabulary: individual characters (in practice raw bytes, so nothing is ever unrepresentable). Then repeat one move: count every adjacent pair of tokens in the corpus, and merge the most frequent pair into a single new token. Each merge grows the vocabulary by one, and frequent fragments assemble themselves:

<BpeFigure />

Run until the vocabulary hits a target size (typically 50,000–250,000 merges), and the effect is elegant: common words ("the", " function") end up as single tokens, rare words split into meaningful chunks — and *anything* can still be encoded, any typo, any language, any binary blob, because the original characters never left the vocabulary. The worst case is simply falling all the way back down to them. Here's a real GPT-style tokenizer on a real sentence; note that the model never sees the letters, only the integer IDs:

<TokenizationFigure />

Tokenization is also the source of a shocking fraction of LLM weirdness, so it's worth internalizing:

- **Models see token IDs, not letters.** "strawberry" may be one opaque token — the model has no direct access to its spelling, which is why counting its r's is famously hard.
- **Numbers get chopped arbitrarily.** `12345` might tokenize as `123` + `45`, which is part of why arithmetic is unnatural for these models.
- **Whitespace matters.** ` function` (with a leading space) and `function` are different tokens; code models are exquisitely sensitive to this.
- **Non-English pays a tax.** Text in less common languages splits into many more tokens per word — literally more expensive, in both context space and API dollars.

Karpathy has a [two-hour video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zduSFxRajkE) building a GPT tokenizer from scratch, which he opens by noting that most "weird LLM behavior" traces back to tokenization. He's right.

## Embeddings: meaning as geometry

A token ID is just an integer — token 5,072 isn't "closer" to token 5,073 in any meaningful sense. The model's first act is to trade the integer for something it can compute with, and the mechanism could not be simpler. The model owns an **embedding matrix**:

$$
\tip{the embedding matrix}{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{\tip{one row per token in the vocabulary}{|V|} \times \tip{the embedding dimension — a few thousand in a frontier model}{d}}
$$

To embed token 5,072, take row 5,072 of $E$. That's the whole operation — a table lookup. (If you prefer Chapter 1's vocabulary: write the token as a one-hot vector and multiply it by $E$; a lookup *is* a matrix multiply by a one-hot.) And the rows of $E$ are parameters like any others: initialized to random numbers, and nudged by backpropagation — every time a token appears in training, the gradient flows into its row — exactly the loop from Chapter 1.

Here is why this solves counting's sharing problem. Under the pressure of next-token prediction, the cheapest way for the model to handle "cat" and "dog" — which appear in nearly identical contexts — is to give them nearly identical vectors, so all the machinery downstream of the embedding handles both for the price of one. Similarity isn't programmed in; it's the *economical solution* gradient descent keeps finding. Evidence about dog-sentences now genuinely transfers to cat-sentences, because to the rest of the network they look almost alike.

The famous demonstration is [word2vec](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781) (2013), which showed that the learned geometry encodes more than similarity — *directions* carry *relationships*:

<EmbeddingSpaceFigure />

The vector from *france* to *paris* is nearly parallel to the one from *italy* to *rome*; the "maleness→royalty" arrows line up the same way, which is why the arithmetic $\vec{\text{king}} - \vec{\text{man}} + \vec{\text{woman}}$ lands near $\vec{\text{queen}}$.

For an engineer, the durable frame is: **the embedding space is the model's universal interface.** A neural network operates on exactly one data type — vectors — and embeddings are the adapters that convert the outside world into it. Words become vectors here; in Chapter 5, image patches and audio frames will arrive through their own encoders as vectors of the same kind. Once something is in the space, "semantically similar" means "geometrically close," and the same downstream machinery can process it no matter what it originally was. This one idea also powers every semantic-search and retrieval product you've used, and it's the foundation the transformer builds on in the next chapter.

## The same loss — and its alter ego

How is all this trained — the predictor, or the embeddings? Both at once, and this is worth being precise about. Everything we've assembled is *one* function: the embedding matrix is the first layer, then comes a network that reads the context's vectors (picture Chapter 1's MLP for now — replacing it is the next chapter's job), then an output layer producing a logit per vocabulary token. Backpropagation doesn't distinguish between these parts. The chain rule walks from the loss back through the output layer, through the network, and on into the embedding rows of whichever tokens appeared in the context — one backward sweep, every parameter nudged by the same gradient. Embeddings aren't a separate preprocessing step that gets trained on its own; they're just the layer nearest the input. (Historically word2vec *did* train embeddings as a standalone artifact; modern models get them as a side effect of the main objective.)

And the loss driving it all, you already know. The vocabulary plays the role of the 10 digits; the correct next token is the one-hot target; the loss is the negative logprob of the truth — cross-entropy, verbatim from Chapter 1. Nothing about the loss changes between MNIST and a frontier LLM except the number of classes. And text has a gift MNIST never had: **the labels are free.** Every position in every sentence on the internet is a training example — the context is the input, and the next token, already sitting right there, is the label. No annotators required; the internet labels itself.

Averaged per token, the loss has an interpretable cousin. **Perplexity** is its exponential:

$$
\mathrm{PPL} = e^{\tip{the average cross-entropy per token}{\bar{L}}}
$$

and it means: the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among $\mathrm{PPL}$ options. Perplexity 1 is omniscience; a model with perplexity 10 on English is hesitating between ten plausible next tokens at each step. When a pre-training paper brags about a loss curve, this is the unit of the y-axis's soul.

<details>
<summary>The alter ego: a language model is a compressor</summary>

Chapter 1 left an IOU: the logprob of a token is, in base 2, the number of **bits** an optimal code needs to encode that token *given the model's beliefs*. A scheme called [arithmetic coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_coding) makes this literal: feed it any probability distribution over next tokens and it encodes text at almost exactly $-\log_2 p$ bits per token — confident correct predictions cost fractions of a bit, surprises cost many.

So "minimize average cross-entropy" and "compress the training data as hard as possible" are *the same objective*, not analogous — identical. A better language model is, mechanically, a better file compressor for text, and vice versa. This equivalence is why researchers sometimes describe pre-training as "compressing the internet" (Chapter 7 takes this framing literally), and why the long-running [Hutter Prize](http://prize.hutter1.net/) treats compressing Wikipedia as an AI benchmark: to compress text optimally, you must model everything that makes it predictable — grammar, facts, style, the works.

## A paragraph of history: the RNN era

Between roughly 2014 and 2018, the dominant neural language models were **recurrent neural networks** (RNNs): read text one token at a time, maintaining a running "hidden state" vector summarizing everything so far. Karpathy's 2015 post [The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks](https://karpathy.github.io/2015/05/21/rnn-effectiveness/) captures the era's excitement — and reading its celebrated samples today is a useful calibration for how far things have come.

RNNs had two fatal flaws. Squeezing an arbitrarily long past into one fixed-size vector meant long-range information got crushed — the model forgot. And processing tokens *sequentially* meant training couldn't be parallelized: token 1,000 couldn't be processed until tokens 1 through 999 had been, no matter how many chips you owned. The architecture that fixed both problems at once — and in doing so, inherited the earth — is the next chapter.
