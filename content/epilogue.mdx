# Epilogue: Where this leaves you

<Byline readingTime={metadata.readingTime} />

The stack, assembled one last time, bottom to top: gradient descent tunes matrices (1) that predict tokens (2) through attention and MLPs (3), made capable by scale (4), efficient by architecture (5), partially legible by interpretability (6); trained on the internet (7), shaped into a character (8), measured imperfectly (9); run on memory-bound silicon (10), served through borrowed systems ideas (11), and finally wrapped in a loop that samples, constrains, and acts (12).

## What isn't solved

This book has been a tour of what works. A fair way to close is a tour of what doesn't — because every chapter, read carefully, ended on a loose thread, and the loose threads are where the field's talent is currently going. If you're looking for where to point your own curiosity, start here:

- **Models that keep learning.** The knowledge cutoff (Chapter 12) is the symptom; the condition is that a model learns once, expensively, and then is frozen — everything after arrives through the context window or not at all. The open problem is *continual learning*: updating weights from experience without catastrophically forgetting (Chapter 8) what's already there, at a cost that doesn't resemble retraining. Until it's solved, "memory" means retrieval plus context engineering — an engineered prosthetic for an ability the organism lacks.
- **Rewards beyond the checkable.** RLVR (Chapter 8) transformed what models can do precisely where answers verify: math, code, and games with clear win conditions. The frontier is everything else: training reasoning on medicine, law, research, and writing requires graders that can't be flattered, for domains where truth isn't a unit test. The active bets — process supervision, which grades the intermediate steps rather than only the final answer; reward models that themselves reason; debate setups — reduce to one question: can models reliably supervise models at tasks humans can't efficiently check? The Constitutional AI bet (Chapter 8: a model critiquing outputs against written principles — one model supervising another) scaled oversight a level; the open question is whether it scales indefinitely.
- **Knowing what it doesn't know.** Hallucination (Chapter 8) is increasingly understood as an *incentive* problem — training and evals reward confident guessing — which makes it fixable in principle: calibrated uncertainty, abstention that scores points, retrieval as grounding. Nobody has fully collected on that promise. A model whose confidence you could trust would let products shed much of their guardrail and verification scaffolding.
- **Reading the weights.** Interpretability (Chapter 6) can extract features and steer with them, but a complete account of "why did the model say that" remains out of reach — and the stakes are rising as chains of thought stop being faithful logs: the reasoning text a model emits doesn't reliably reflect the computation that produced its answer (Chapters 6, 12). The field's trajectory, from neurons to features to circuits, points toward something like auditing: checking a model for deception the way you'd check a binary for malware. Interpretability remains the open problem in computer science with the highest ratio of importance to people working on it.
- **The data wall, still standing.** Chapter 4's arithmetic hasn't changed: compute scales, the public internet doesn't. Synthetic data works but must dodge model collapse, the degradation that compounds when models train on their own outputs. The deeper embarrassment is sample efficiency: a human learns language from roughly a hundred million words (a developmental-linguistics commonplace), while a frontier model needs a hundred thousand times more (Chapter 4's token counts). No datacenter purchase compares to closing that gap, and nobody yet knows what will.
- **Escaping the quadratic — and the treadmill.** Attention's cost (Chapter 3) and decode's memory-boundedness (Chapter 10) are both artifacts of one architecture's shape. State-space hybrids (Chapter 5) attack the first with constant-size state and linear-time inference; so far they've been absorbed as hybrid layers rather than winning outright — and the next architecture, if there is one, probably arrives the same way: absorbed, not victorious.
- **Generation that isn't left-to-right.** Diffusion language models (Chapter 11) generate a whole block in parallel and refine it iteratively — a serving play (dozens of weight-reads instead of thousands) with a deeper consequence: a generator that can *revise* has no autoregressive original sin (Chapter 12). Early mistakes get edited rather than disavowed, and infilling and editing become native operations instead of retrofits. The open questions are whether quality holds at frontier scale, and whether revision-native generation changes how models *reason* as well as how fast they emit.
- **How small can good get?** The scaling story ran one direction; the counter-current matters as much. Over-training (Chapter 4), distillation (Chapter 11), and 4-bit quantization (Chapter 11) keep compressing yesterday's frontier into something that runs on a phone — private, offline, latency-free, and beyond any provider's terms of service. The research questions underneath: is there a floor — how few parameters can carry general competence? How much of a trillion-parameter model is *knowledge*, which retrieval (Chapter 12) could supply from outside instead? The practical frontier is hybrid — a local model for the routine 90%, escalation to the datacenter for the rest — and it's as much a product problem as a research one.
- **Better learning than backprop?** Everything in this book rests on Chapter 1's engine, and the engine has real costs: the backward pass stores every activation, updates are globally synchronous — every weight waits for the full round trip — and nothing like it appears to run in brains, which learn from vastly less data on twenty watts. The challengers — Hinton's forward-forward algorithm, which replaces the backward pass with two forward passes judged against each other; predictive coding; local rules that update each weight from nearby signals only — share one theme: learn without a global backward pass. None is competitive at scale yet — the bitter-lesson irony being that backprop *is* the general method that scales. But two pressures keep the search honest: optimizer progress like Muon (Chapter 7) proves the training algorithm still has headroom, and analog or neuromorphic hardware, where exact gradients don't physically exist, would *require* a backprop-free rule. Whoever finds one that scales rewrites Chapter 1.
- **Agents that don't derail — or get hijacked.** The gap between demo and dependable is post-training for long horizons (error compounding is the autoregressive original sin stretched over hours — Chapter 12), context engineering as a real systems discipline, and above all security: prompt injection remains unsolved, and agents keep getting more tools. The industry is currently building the '90s web's security story in fast-forward; the engineer who brings actual security discipline to this layer will not lack for work.

## Two closing convictions

Honestly held. First: the bitter lesson keeps winning, so hold your clever scaffolding loosely — a surprising amount of 2024's agent engineering became 2026's post-training, absorbed into the weights (test-time compute, better RL, longer horizons are the current frontier, and they will eat parts of this book too — that's what the list above is *for*). Second: everything in this book was built by people reasoning from a handful of primitives you now have — prediction, gradients, attention, scale, and a loop. The field is young, the papers are public, and the distance from "read the primer" to "contribute" has never been shorter.

Go build something.
