# Epilogue: Where this leaves you

The stack, assembled one last time, bottom to top: gradient descent tunes matrices (1) that predict tokens (2) through attention and MLPs (3), made capable by scale (4), efficient by architecture (5), partially legible by interpretability (6); trained on the internet (7), shaped into a character (8), measured imperfectly (9); run on memory-bound silicon (10), served through borrowed systems ideas (11), and finally wrapped in a loop that samples, constrains, and acts (12).

## What isn't solved

This book has been a tour of what works. A fair way to close is a tour of what doesn't — because every chapter, read carefully, ended on a loose thread, and the loose threads are where the field's talent is currently going. If you're looking for where to point your own curiosity, start here:

- **Models that keep learning.** The knowledge cutoff (Chapter 12) is a symptom of something deeper: a model learns once, expensively, and then is frozen — everything after arrives through the context window or not at all. The open problem is *continual learning*: updating weights from experience without catastrophically forgetting (Chapter 8) what's already there, at a cost that doesn't resemble retraining. Until it's solved, "memory" means retrieval plus context engineering — an engineered prosthetic for an ability the organism lacks.
- **Rewards beyond the checkable.** RLVR (Chapter 8) transformed what models can do precisely where answers verify — math, code, games. The frontier is everything else: training reasoning on medicine, law, research, and writing requires graders that can't be flattered, for domains where truth isn't a unit test. The active bets — process supervision (grade the steps, not just the answer), reward models that themselves reason, debate and critique setups — all reduce to one question: can models reliably supervise models at tasks humans can't efficiently check? The Constitutional AI bet (Chapter 8) scaled oversight one level; the open question is whether it scales indefinitely.
- **Knowing what it doesn't know.** Hallucination (Chapter 8) is increasingly understood as an *incentive* problem — training and evals reward confident guessing — which makes it fixable in principle: calibrated uncertainty, abstention that scores points, retrieval as grounding. Nobody has fully collected on that promise. A model whose confidence you could trust would change how every product in this stack is built — fewer guardrails, fewer verifiers, fewer judges.
- **Reading the weights.** Interpretability (Chapter 6) can extract features and steer with them, but a complete account of "why did the model say that" remains out of reach — and the stakes are rising as transcripts stop being faithful logs (Chapters 6, 12). The field's trajectory — from neurons to features to circuits — points toward something like auditing: checking a model for deception the way you'd check a binary for malware. It remains, as Chapter 6 said, the open problem with the best importance-to-people ratio in computer science.
- **The data wall, still standing.** Chapter 4's arithmetic hasn't changed: compute scales, the public internet doesn't. Synthetic data works but must dodge the snake-eating-its-tail failure mode; the deeper embarrassment is sample efficiency — a human learns language from roughly a hundred million words, a frontier model needs a hundred thousand times more. Whatever closes that gap is worth more than any datacenter.
- **Escaping the quadratic — and the treadmill.** Attention's cost (Chapter 3) and decode's memory-boundedness (Chapter 10) are both artifacts of one architecture's shape, and the challengers aim at each: state-space hybrids for linear-time context (Chapter 5), diffusion language models for parallel generation (Chapter 11). None has displaced the transformer; all of them have changed what "transformer" ships with. The next architecture, if there is one, probably arrives the same way — absorbed, not victorious.
- **Agents that don't derail — or get hijacked.** The gap between demo and dependable is post-training for long horizons (error compounding is the autoregressive original sin stretched over hours — Chapter 12), context engineering as a real systems discipline, and above all security: prompt injection remains unsolved, and agents keep getting more tools. The industry is currently building the '90s web's security story in fast-forward; the engineer who brings actual security discipline to this layer will not lack for work.

## Two closing convictions

Honestly held. First: the bitter lesson keeps winning, so hold your clever scaffolding loosely — a surprising amount of 2024's agent engineering became 2026's post-training, absorbed into the weights (test-time compute, better RL, longer horizons are the current frontier, and they will eat parts of this book too — that's what the list above is *for*). Second: everything in this book was built by people reasoning from a handful of primitives you now have — prediction, gradients, attention, scale, and a loop. The field is young, the papers are public, and the distance from "read the primer" to "contribute" has never been shorter.

Go build something.
