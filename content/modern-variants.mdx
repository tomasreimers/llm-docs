# Modern variants: What's in a frontier model

If you diff GPT-2 (2019) against a frontier open model like [DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437) (2025), the shocking thing is how little the skeleton changed: embeddings, attention, MLPs, residual stream, next-token head. But the config file grew some new fields, and each one is a response to a specific pressure — almost always either *the cost of the MLPs* or *the cost of the KV cache*.

So let's do the diff by hand. At the end of Chapter 3 we counted GPT-2's 124M parameters down to the matrix: 12 blocks of (2.4M attention + 4.7M MLP) ≈ 85M, plus a 38.6M embedding matrix. That fully-itemized model is our starting point, and this chapter upgrades it one config field at a time, redoing the arithmetic after each change:

1. **Mixture of experts** makes the MLPs — the majority of the parameters — *sparse*: huge total knowledge, small per-token cost.
2. **Attention variants** (GQA, MLA) shrink the KV cache, the data structure that dominates inference memory.
3. **RoPE and systems tricks** stretch the context window from GPT-2's 1,024 tokens toward a million.
4. **Multimodality** turns out to require almost nothing new: convert everything into tokens and reuse the whole machine.

By the end, the little model from Chapter 3 will read, field for field, like a frontier config.

## Mixture of experts: sparse MLPs

Start with the MLPs, because that's where the parameters are: 4.7M of each GPT-2 block's 7.1M — two-thirds. Chapter 4 said more parameters mean lower loss; the tension is that in a dense model every parameter you add makes *every token* more expensive, because all the weights run all the time. Your brain does not work this way — you don't engage your knowledge of French cooking while debugging a segfault.

**Mixture of experts** (MoE) makes the MLP sparse. Instead of one MLP per block, have many (8 to 256 in practice) — the "experts" — plus a small learned **router**: a linear layer that reads the token's vector from the residual stream, scores every expert (a softmax, so the scores are weights that sum to 1), and sends the token through only the top-k (often 2 to 8). The chosen experts' outputs are combined as a **weighted sum**, using the winners' router weights renormalized to sum to 1 — for the figure's last token, $\text{output} = 0.62 \cdot \text{expert}_2(x) + 0.38 \cdot \text{expert}_5(x)$ — and written back to the stream. The weighted sum isn't just bookkeeping: because the router's weights sit *inside* the output, gradients flow back through them, which is the only reason a routing decision can be learned by backpropagation at all:

Now upgrade GPT-2 and redo Chapter 3's arithmetic. Replace each block's single 4.7M-parameter MLP with 8 experts of the same shape, route top-2:

- **Total parameters:** each block is now 2.4M attention + 8 × 4.7M experts ≈ 40M; times 12 blocks plus embeddings ≈ **520M**. The model *knows* 4× more.
- **Active parameters:** each token runs 2.4M attention + 2 × 4.7M chosen experts ≈ 12M per block — **180M** across the model. Each token *pays* only ~1.5× the dense cost.

That asymmetry — 4.2× the knowledge for 1.45× the per-token compute — is the entire pitch, and it scales: DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total parameters but only ~37B active per token. GPT-4-class knowledge at mid-size-model FLOPs. First scaled by Google's [Switch Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961) and pushed into the open by [Mixtral](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04088), MoE is now effectively universal at the frontier.

The catch is engineering, not math. The router is trained end-to-end like every other weight, and left alone it will happily collapse onto a few favorite experts — a rich-get-richer loop: a slightly-preferred expert gets more tokens, so it trains more, so it gets preferred more, until most experts are dead weight. The standard fix is to make imbalance *part of the loss*: add an auxiliary term that grows when routing is uneven, so gradient descent itself trades a sliver of prediction quality for spreading tokens out. And since hundreds of experts can't fit on one GPU, they're sharded across many, which turns every MoE layer into a *network* problem: tokens physically travel to their experts and back (a preview of Chapter 10).

A useful demystification to close on: despite the name, experts don't specialize into "the biology expert." The Mixtral team checked — they ran documents from different domains (code, math papers, biology papers) through the model and plotted which experts fired, expecting topical clustering. Routing was essentially uniform across domains. What they found instead was *syntactic* and *token-level* specialization: one expert consistently catching whitespace and indentation in code, others gravitating to numbers, punctuation, or particular keywords, with consecutive tokens often routed to the same expert. In hindsight the result makes sense twice over. The router makes its choice per token, per layer — a single token passing through DeepSeek-V3 triggers hundreds of independent routing decisions, so there is no single place where "this is a biology question" could even be decided. And the load balancer from the previous paragraph actively *fights* topical specialization: if one expert owned biology, every biology paper would swamp it and the auxiliary loss would push the traffic apart. "Expert" is a term of art for *a shard of MLP capacity*, not a specialist — the specialization that survives training is the kind that's spread evenly through text: syntax, not subject matter. Discovered, rather than assigned.

<details>
<summary>The load-balancing loss, concretely.</summary>

The Switch Transformer's version, still the canonical one. For each expert $i$, measure two things over a batch: $f_i$, the *fraction of tokens actually routed* to it, and $P_i$, the router's *average probability* on it. Add to the training loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \; = \; \alpha \cdot n_{\text{experts}} \cdot \sum_i \, \tip{fraction of tokens routed to expert i}{f_i} \cdot \tip{average router probability on expert i}{P_i}
$$

A product of two quantities that each sum to 1 is minimized when both are spread uniformly — collapse onto one expert and the term spikes, so the gradient pushes the router's scores apart. ($f_i$ isn't differentiable — it's a count — which is why $P_i$ is in the product: the gradient flows through the probabilities.) The coefficient $\alpha$ is kept small (~0.01): enough pressure to keep every expert alive, not enough to override genuinely useful routing. DeepSeek-V3 went a step further with *auxiliary-loss-free* balancing — instead of a loss term, each expert gets a bias added to its routing score only, nudged up when the expert is underused and down when overloaded — rebalancing the traffic without distorting the gradients at all.

</details>

## Taming the KV cache: GQA, MQA, MLA

Next, attention — but the problem isn't the attention *weights* (2.4M per block; they're cheap). It's a data structure that doesn't exist in the config at all. Recall the seed from Chapter 3 — the spreadsheet, one more time: generating appends a column and computes *only* it, because every finished token's keys and values are cached rather than recomputed:

<KvGridFigure />

Now put a price on that convenience. Each cached token stores a key vector and a value vector *per layer* — one K/V pair for every cell in its column — and in Chapter 3's multi-head attention those span the full model width. In half-precision (2 bytes per number):

$$
\tip{cache bytes per token of context}{\text{bytes}} \; = \; \tip{a key and a value}{2} \times \tip{number of layers}{n_{\text{layer}}} \times \tip{number of KV heads}{n_{\text{kv}}} \times \tip{dimensions per head}{d_{\text{head}}} \times \tip{bytes per number in half precision}{2}
$$

For our GPT-2 (12 layers, 12 heads of 64 dimensions): $2 \times 12 \times 12 \times 64 \times 2 = 37$ KB per token — at its 1,024-token context, a trivial 38 MB. Nobody at OpenAI lost sleep over this in 2019. Now scale it. Llama 3 70B's shape is 80 layers, 64 heads of 128 dimensions: $2 \times 80 \times 64 \times 128 \times 2 = 2.6$ **MB** per token — and a single 128K-token conversation needs **320 GB**, four H100s' worth of memory, for one user's cache. Since (Chapter 10 preview) inference speed is mostly determined by how many bytes you move through memory, the KV cache directly limits context length, batch size, and cost. The cache grew from rounding error to the single most expensive object in the datacenter.

The fixes form a tidy progression — keep all 64 *query* heads, but let them share a smaller set of K/V heads (only $n_{\text{kv}}$ enters the formula; queries are never cached):

- **[Multi-query attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02150)** (MQA): one K/V head shared by all query heads. The formula's $n_{\text{kv}}$ drops 64 → 1: cache shrinks 64×; quality measurably dips.
- **[Grouped-query attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13245)** (GQA): the compromise — groups of query heads share K/V heads. Llama 3 70B runs 64 query heads against 8 KV heads: 8× smaller cache, negligible quality loss. This is today's default.
- **Multi-head latent attention** (MLA): DeepSeek's move — store only a small learned *compression* of all the heads' keys and values (a 576-dimensional latent per token per layer) and decompress on the fly. Trades a little compute (abundant) for a lot of memory (scarce), which is the right direction of trade.

If you only remember one thing from this section: when a model card advertises some attention variant, it is almost always really an announcement about *KV cache economics*.

## Long context: RoPE and its stretching exercises

Frontier context windows went from GPT-2's 1,024 tokens to 1M+ in about five years. Two things had to happen.

First, **positions had to generalize.** Chapter 3's answer to word order — and GPT-2's — was to *add* a learned position vector to each embedding: position 7 gets the position-7 vector, stored in a 1,024 × 768 table (0.8M parameters of our ledger). That table is two problems in one: the model has simply never seen a vector for position 5,000, and "these tokens are 3 apart" — the thing attention usually cares about — is only represented indirectly. **RoPE** ([Rotary Position Embedding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09864)), today's standard, deletes the table entirely (our ledger's one *negative* line item: −0.8M parameters, replaced by a formula with none). It encodes position by *rotating* each query and key vector by an angle proportional to its position. The trick is what rotation does to attention scores: a dot product depends only on the angle *between* two vectors, so the score between a query and a key depends only on how far apart they are — relative position, baked into the geometry:

<RopeFigure />

<details>
<summary>RoPE in coordinates, and why it stretches.</summary>

Pair up the dimensions of each query and key — a $d$-dimensional vector becomes $d/2$ little 2D planes — and rotate the pair in plane $i$ at position $m$ by angle $m\theta_i$, where the frequencies $\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$ span from fast (first pairs: sensitive to adjacent-token offsets) to extremely slow (last pairs: tracking document-scale distances). It's the same idea as a clock: a second hand, a minute hand, and an hour hand together tell you both fine and coarse time. Because the encoding is a *formula* rather than a lookup table, it extends to unseen positions — and context-extension methods like [YaRN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00071) work by rescaling those frequencies so a model trained at 8K positions can operate at 128K after a comparatively cheap fine-tune (done in "mid-training" — Chapter 7). This is why long context arrived as point releases rather than retrains.

</details>

Second, **the quadratic had to be survivable.** Attention still compares every token to every token — at 1M tokens, that's $10^{12}$ score pairs per head per layer, naively materialized as a terabyte-scale matrix. [FlashAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135) computes *exact* attention but reorders the computation so the big seq×seq score matrix never materializes in slow GPU memory — a pure systems win, and arguably the most influential systems paper of the era (the full why belongs to Chapter 10). On top of that, long-context models interleave **sliding-window layers**: most layers attend only to a local window of recent tokens, and only some layers attend globally.

A consumer warning from the evals world: "supports 1M tokens" means the model *runs* at 1M tokens. Whether it can actually *use* information spread across that window (measured by needle-in-a-haystack and multi-hop retrieval evals) is a separate and often disappointing question — see Chapter 9.

## The little diffs: norms and activations

Two renames remain before our upgraded GPT-2's config reads fully modern. **RMSNorm** replaces LayerNorm (the same magnitude-stabilizing job from Chapter 3, computed more cheaply), applied *before* each block rather than after (pre-norm; trains more stably). **SwiGLU** replaces ReLU in the MLPs (a gated, smoother nonlinearity that wins consistently for reasons nobody finds deeply satisfying). Neither changes the story; file under: the field ran an enormous ablation study for a decade, and these won.

## Multimodality: everything becomes a token

Frontier models see images, hear audio, and increasingly emit both. The remarkable fact is how little new machinery this required — the recipe is to convert other modalities *into tokens* and reuse the entire apparatus:

1. A **vision encoder** — a [Vision Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11929) (ViT) — chops the image into a grid of patches (16×16 pixels each in the original), flattens each patch, and treats it exactly like a token embedding. No convolutions; the same blocks as Chapter 3, usually without the causal mask (it's an encoder — Chapter 3's lineage, reading not writing).
2. A small **adapter** projects the encoder's output vectors into the language model's embedding space.
3. The LLM proceeds as if the image were a few hundred words it had read. ([CLIP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020) established the shared text-image space that makes this alignment work; audio follows the same pattern with a speech encoder.)

<VitFigure />

Early multimodal systems bolted encoders onto finished LLMs; current frontier models are *natively multimodal* — trained on mixed text/image/audio streams from the start. But the deep point stands: the transformer never knew it was a language model. It's a sequence model, and everything — prose, code, screenshots, speech — is sequences of embeddings to it.

## Sidebar: the road not (yet) taken

The transformer's quadratic attention keeps inspiring challengers — most prominently **state-space models** like [Mamba](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752), which are, with real irony, a modernized recurrence: constant-size state, linear-time inference, the very design attention replaced in Chapter 3's opening. They now match transformers at moderate scale, and ideas from them show up in production as *hybrids* (a few attention layers among many linear-time layers). Worth watching; not yet worth reorganizing your mental model around. The transformer's real moat is nine years of tooling, kernels, and hardware co-evolution — a lesson any engineer who has tried to replace a battle-tested system will recognize.

Step back and look at what we built. We took Chapter 3's 124M-parameter GPT-2 and, field by field: made its MLPs sparse (520M total, 180M active), shared its KV heads (cache ÷8), swapped its position table for rotations (context extendable), renamed its norms and activations, and taught it to eat pixels. At no point did we touch the skeleton. That is the state of the art: the 2017 architecture, relentlessly cost-optimized.
