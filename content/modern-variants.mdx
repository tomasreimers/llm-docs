# Modern variants: What's in a frontier model

<Byline readingTime={metadata.readingTime} />

If you diff GPT-2 (2019) against a frontier open model like [DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437) (2025), the shocking thing is how little the skeleton changed: embeddings, attention, MLPs, residual stream, next-token head. But the config file grew some new fields, and each one is a response to a specific pressure — almost always either *the cost of the MLPs* or *the cost of the KV cache* (the stored keys and values of earlier tokens — defined mid-chapter).

So let's do the diff by hand, starting from Chapter 3's fully-itemized GPT-2 — 12 blocks of (2.4M attention + 4.7M MLP) ≈ 85M, plus 38.6M of embeddings = 124M — and upgrading one config field at a time, redoing the arithmetic after each change:

1. **Mixture of experts** makes the MLPs — the majority of the parameters — *sparse*: huge total knowledge, small per-token cost.
2. **Attention variants** — grouped-query and latent attention (GQA, MLA) — shrink the KV cache, the data structure that dominates inference memory.
3. **RoPE** (rotary position embeddings) **and systems tricks** stretch the context window from GPT-2's 1,024 tokens toward a million.
4. **Multimodality** turns out to require almost nothing new: convert everything into tokens and reuse the whole machine.

By the end, the little model from Chapter 3 will read, field for field, like a frontier config.

## Mixture of experts: sparse MLPs

Start with the MLPs, because that's where the parameters are: 4.7M of each GPT-2 block's 7.1M — two-thirds. Chapter 4 said more parameters mean lower loss; the tension is that in a dense model every parameter you add makes *every token* more expensive, because all the weights run all the time. Your brain doesn't work this way: French cooking stays off while you debug a segfault.

**Mixture of experts** (MoE) makes the MLP sparse. Instead of one MLP per block, have many (8 to 256 in practice), the "experts," plus a small learned **router**: a linear layer that reads the token's vector from the residual stream (Chapter 3's running per-token bus), scores every expert (a softmax, so the scores are weights that sum to 1), and sends the token through only the top-k (often 2 to 8). The chosen experts' outputs are combined as a **weighted sum**, using the winners' router weights renormalized to sum to 1 (for the figure's last token, $\text{output} = 0.62 \cdot \text{expert}_2(x) + 0.38 \cdot \text{expert}_5(x)$) and written back to the stream. The weighted sum is what makes routing trainable: a top-k pick is discrete, with no derivative — but the router's probabilities sit *inside* the output as multipliers, so gradients flow back through them and the discrete pick rides on a differentiable score:

<MoeFigure />

Now upgrade GPT-2 and redo Chapter 3's arithmetic. Replace each block's single 4.7M-parameter MLP with 8 experts of the same shape, route top-2:

- **Total parameters:** each block is now 2.4M attention + 8 × 4.7M experts ≈ 40M; times 12 blocks plus embeddings ≈ **520M**. The model *knows* 4× more.
- **Active parameters:** each token runs 2.4M attention + 2 × 4.7M chosen experts ≈ 12M per block; times 12 blocks plus the 38.6M embeddings ≈ **180M** across the model. Each token *pays* only ~1.5× the dense cost (per-token FLOPs track the parameters a token actually touches — Chapter 4's 2 FLOPs per weight).

That asymmetry — 4.2× the knowledge for 1.45× the per-token compute — scales: DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total parameters but only ~37B active per token. GPT-4-class knowledge at mid-size-model FLOPs. First scaled by Google's [Switch Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961) and pushed into the open by [Mixtral](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04088), MoE is now effectively universal at the frontier.

The catch is engineering. The router is trained end-to-end like every other weight, and left alone it will happily collapse onto a few favorite experts — a rich-get-richer loop: a slightly-preferred expert gets more tokens, trains more, gets preferred more, until most experts are dead weight. The standard fix is to make imbalance *part of the loss*: add an auxiliary term that grows when routing is uneven, so gradient descent itself trades a sliver of prediction quality for spreading tokens out. And because experts are sharded across GPUs, every MoE layer is also a *network* hop: tokens physically travel to their experts and back (Chapter 10).

One demystification: despite the name, experts don't specialize into "the biology expert." The Mixtral team checked: expecting topical clustering across code, math, and biology documents, they found routing essentially uniform across domains. What they found instead was *syntactic* and *token-level* specialization: one expert consistently catching whitespace and indentation in code, others gravitating to numbers, punctuation, or particular keywords, with consecutive tokens often routed to the same expert. In hindsight the result makes sense twice over. The router chooses per token, per layer (hundreds of independent decisions per token in DeepSeek-V3), so there is no single place where "this is a biology question" could even be decided. And the load balancer from the previous paragraph actively *fights* topical specialization: if one expert owned biology, every biology paper would swamp it and the auxiliary loss would push the traffic apart. "Expert" is a term of art for a shard of MLP capacity; the specialization that survives training is the kind spread evenly through text — syntax — and nobody assigned it.

<details>
<summary>The load-balancing loss, concretely.</summary>

The Switch Transformer's version, still the canonical one. For each expert $i$, measure two things over a batch: $f_i$, the *fraction of tokens actually routed* to it, and $P_i$, the router's *average probability* on it. Add to the training loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \; = \; \alpha \cdot n_{\text{experts}} \cdot \sum_i \, \tip{fraction of tokens routed to expert i}{f_i} \cdot \tip{average router probability on expert i}{P_i}
$$

A product of two quantities that each sum to 1 is minimized when both are spread uniformly — collapse onto one expert and the term spikes, so the gradient pushes the router's scores apart. ($f_i$ is a count, not differentiable, which is why $P_i$ is in the product: the gradient flows through the probabilities.) The coefficient $\alpha$ is kept small (~0.01): enough pressure to keep every expert alive, not enough to override genuinely useful routing. DeepSeek-V3 went a step further with *auxiliary-loss-free* balancing: instead of a loss term, each expert gets a bias added to its routing score only, nudged up when the expert is underused and down when overloaded, rebalancing the traffic without distorting the gradients at all.

</details>

## Taming the KV cache: GQA, MQA, MLA

Next, attention — but the problem isn't the attention *weights* (2.4M per block; they're cheap). To see what the problem actually is, walk back through how attention works, from Chapter 3.

The mechanism, replayed from Chapter 3: each token's vector gets projected into a **query** ("what am I looking for?"), a **key** ("what do I contain?"), and a **value** ("what I hand over if matched"). A token attends by dotting its query against the key of *every token before it*, softmaxing the scores into weights, and taking the weighted average of their values. And each layer runs that lookup many times in parallel, in **heads**. A head is an independent copy of the whole query/key/value machinery working in its own slice of the dimensions: our GPT-2 splits its 768-wide vectors into 12 heads of 64 dimensions, each with its own small $W_q, W_k, W_v$, so each can hunt for a different relationship — one tracking pronouns to their referents, another watching the previous token (Chapter 3's head gallery). Each head's lookup returns its own 64-dimensional weighted average of values; the 12 results are concatenated back to 768 wide, mixed through one output matrix, and *that* is what attention adds to the residual stream — which the MLP reads next. Every head produces, and must keep, **its own keys and values**. So far, nothing new.

The bill arrives at generation time. The model emits one token at a time, and each *new* token's query must search the keys — and fetch the values — of every token that came before it, in every head, in every layer. Those keys and values aren't new: token 50's K/V are the same when token 51 looks at them as when token 500 does. Recomputing them at every step makes generating a whole sequence cost quadratically; computing each token's K/V once, when that token is produced, and *storing* them is what everyone does. (The cache kills the redundant projection work; the query-against-every-stored-key score comparisons remain, which is why long context stays expensive below.) That store is the **KV cache**, and the spreadsheet from Chapter 3 is a picture of exactly this: generating appends a column and computes only it, because every finished column's K/V sit in the cache:

<KvGridFigure />

Storage isn't free: the cache has to live in GPU memory, next to the weights, for as long as the conversation does. Put a price on it. Each cached token stores one key vector and one value vector per head, per layer — and in Chapter 3's multi-head attention, the heads' K/V together span the full model width. In half-precision (2 bytes per number):

$$
\tip{cache bytes per token of context}{\text{bytes}} \; = \; \tip{a key and a value}{2} \times \tip{number of layers}{n_{\text{layer}}} \times \tip{number of KV heads}{n_{\text{kv}}} \times \tip{dimensions per head}{d_{\text{head}}} \times \tip{bytes per number in half precision}{2}
$$

For our GPT-2 (12 layers, 12 heads of 64 dimensions): $2 \times 12 \times 12 \times 64 \times 2 = 37$ KB per token — at its 1,024-token context, a trivial 38 MB. Nobody at OpenAI lost sleep over this in 2019. Now scale it. Llama 3 70B's shape is 80 layers, 64 heads of 128 dimensions: $2 \times 80 \times 64 \times 128 \times 2 = 2.6$ **MB** per token — and a single 128K-token conversation needs 2.6 MB × 131,072 ≈ **340 GB** (more than four 80-GB H100s) for one user's cache. Since inference speed is mostly set by bytes moved through memory (Chapter 10), the KV cache directly limits context length, batch size, and cost: it grew from rounding error to the most expensive object in the datacenter.

The fixes form a progression: keep all 64 *query* heads, but let them share a smaller set of K/V heads — each query head still asks its own question through its own $W_q$, but several search over the *same* keys and values, computed by one K/V head:

<KvSharingFigure />

Since only the K/V heads are cached (queries are used once and discarded — nothing ever looks *back* at a query), $n_{\text{kv}}$ is the only head count in the cache formula, and shrinking it shrinks the cache proportionally:

- **[Multi-query attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02150)** (MQA): one K/V head shared by all query heads. The formula's $n_{\text{kv}}$ drops 64 → 1: cache shrinks 64×; quality dips (the GQA paper measures the gap).
- **[Grouped-query attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13245)** (GQA): the compromise — groups of query heads share K/V heads. Llama 3 70B runs 64 query heads against 8 KV heads: 8× smaller cache, negligible quality loss. This is today's default.
- **Multi-head latent attention** (MLA): DeepSeek's move, and a departure from head-sharing — store only a small learned *compression* of all the heads' keys and values (in [DeepSeek-V2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434), a 576-dimensional latent per token per layer, where its 128 heads of dimension 128 would otherwise cache $2 \times 128 \times 128 = 32{,}768$ — a 57× compression) and decompress on the fly. That spends compute to save memory: backwards for training, where compute is the binding cost (Chapter 4), exactly right for generation, where the arithmetic units idle waiting on memory (Chapter 10 makes this precise).

<KvVariantsFigure />

A model card that advertises some attention variant is almost always really announcing *KV-cache economics*.

## Long context: RoPE and its stretching exercises

Frontier context windows went from GPT-2's 1,024 tokens to 1M+ in about five years. Two things had to happen.

First, **positions had to generalize.** Chapter 3's answer to word order (and GPT-2's) was to *add* a learned position vector to each embedding: position 7 gets the position-7 vector, stored in a 1,024 × 768 table (0.8M parameters of our ledger). That table is two problems in one: the model has never seen a vector for position 5,000, and "these tokens are 3 apart" (what attention actually cares about) is only represented indirectly. **RoPE** ([Rotary Position Embedding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09864)), today's standard, deletes the table entirely (our ledger's one *negative* line item: −0.8M parameters, replaced by a formula with none). It encodes position by *rotating* each query and key vector by an angle proportional to its position. The trick is what rotation does to attention scores. Rotation preserves length, and a dot product depends on lengths and the angle between — so when query and key rotate *together*, each by its own position's angle, absolute positions cancel and only the difference survives. The score sees content plus relative offset, never "position #4,821" — relative position, baked into the geometry:

<RopeFigure />

<details>
<summary>RoPE in coordinates, and why it stretches.</summary>

Pair up the dimensions of each query and key (a $d$-dimensional vector becomes $d/2$ little 2D planes) and rotate the pair in plane $i$ at position $m$ by angle $m\theta_i$, where the frequencies $\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$ range from fast (a big angle per position step) to extremely slow (thousands of positions per degree). Why have both? Because heads have two opposite kinds of job. Some matching needs the exact nearby offset to matter: a head tracking "the token *right before* me" must score offset 1 differently from offset 2, which takes a **fast** plane, where one step swings the angle sharply; in a slow plane, offsets 1 and 5 look almost identical. Other matching needs distance *not* to matter: "find the definition of this variable from 3,000 tokens back" is a content match that must survive any offset: that takes a **slow** plane, which barely moves over 3,000 steps; in a fast plane, 3,000 steps is hundreds of full revolutions, and the scrambled phase would destroy the match. And the full geometric spread is needed because any single frequency *aliases* (angles wrap around, so many different offsets land on the same angle) while many frequencies together pin offsets down unambiguously. It's exactly a clock: the second hand alone can't distinguish 3:00:15 from 4:00:15, the hour hand alone can't distinguish 3:00:15 from 3:00:45; together they're unambiguous. The *whole* vector rotates — no dedicated "position slot," every dimension carrying content and every pair spinning at its own speed (the slowest planes are so nearly frozen they behave like an unrotated region in practice). (A few "partial rotary" implementations rotate only a fraction of the dimensions; standard RoPE rotates everything.) Because the encoding is a *formula* rather than a lookup table, it extends to unseen positions — and context-extension methods like [YaRN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00071) work by rescaling those frequencies so a model trained at 8K positions can operate at 128K after a comparatively cheap fine-tune (done in "mid-training" — Chapter 7). This is why long context arrived as point releases rather than retrains.

An objection: Chapter 2 established that *directions carry meaning* — so doesn't rotating a vector by its position scramble its meaning? Three facts dissolve it. First, RoPE never touches the residual stream: it rotates only the queries and keys inside an attention head, in the instant before their dot product; the values, the stream, and everything the MLP reads are unrotated. Second, queries and keys rotate *together*, so what the score sees is content plus relative angle; the comparison geometry survives even as absolute directions spin. Third, the model learns $W_q$ and $W_k$ with the rotation already in place (a fixed, known transform) and learns to *exploit* it: position-blind matching gets parked in the nearly-frozen slow planes, position-sensitive work in the fast ones. The rotation isn't something the model fights; it's a coordinate system the model grows into.

</details>

Second, **the quadratic had to be survivable.** Attention still compares every token to every token — at 1M tokens, that's $10^{12}$ score pairs per head per layer, naively materialized as a terabyte-scale matrix. [FlashAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135) computes *exact* attention but reorders the computation so the big seq×seq score matrix never materializes in slow GPU memory — a pure systems win that every frontier stack now ships (the full why belongs to Chapter 10). On top of that, long-context models interleave **sliding-window layers**: most layers attend only to a local window of recent tokens, and only some layers attend globally. (Information still crosses windows: stacked local layers each carry it one window further, and the occasional global layer is the long-range relay.)

A consumer warning from the evals world: "supports 1M tokens" means the model *runs* at 1M tokens. Whether it can actually *use* information spread across that window (measured by needle-in-a-haystack tests — hide one fact in a long document, ask for it back — and multi-hop retrieval evals) is a separate and often disappointing question — see Chapter 9.

## The little diffs: norms and activations

Two renames remain before our upgraded GPT-2's config reads fully modern — and since Chapter 3 gave normalization a single clause, here is what it actually is.

Start with the job. The residual stream is pure accumulation — every attention step and MLP *adds*, nothing scales back down — so stack 80 blocks and magnitudes drift, and drift compounds through every weight matrix: too large and activations and gradients blow up, too small and they fade out (Chapter 3's vanishing-gradient aside, at depth 80). **Normalization** is the thermostat: before each block reads the stream, rescale the token's vector to a standard size, so every block at every depth sees inputs in the same predictable range. **LayerNorm**, the 2017 original, does it per token: take the vector's $d$ entries, subtract their mean, divide by their standard deviation, then let the model re-scale each dimension with learned parameters (the $\odot$ below is elementwise multiplication):

$$
\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x) \; = \; \tip{a learned per-dimension gain}{\gamma} \odot \frac{x - \tip{the mean of the vector's d entries}{\mu}}{\tip{the standard deviation of those entries}{\sigma}} + \tip{a learned per-dimension shift}{\beta}
$$

**RMSNorm** is what a decade of ablations (remove-one-component-and-remeasure experiments) left standing: it turns out the mean-subtraction and the shift $\beta$ barely matter, so drop them — just divide the vector by the root-mean-square of its entries and apply the learned gains. Same stabilizing effect, cheaper to compute. Modern models also move *where* the norm sits: the 2017 transformer normalized after each residual addition (post-norm); modern models normalize the copy of the stream being fed *into* attention or the MLP and leave the stream itself untouched (**pre-norm**) — preserving the clean additive highway from Chapter 3's residual-stream section, which improves training stability at depth.

**SwiGLU** (Swish-Gated Linear Unit) replaces ReLU inside the MLPs. Recall Chapter 1's ReLU: $\max(0, x)$, a hard on/off gate. SwiGLU makes the gate *learned and smooth*: the MLP's expansion layer produces two projections instead of one (call them the **content** and the **gate**), and the output is their elementwise product, $\text{content} \odot \mathrm{SiLU}(\text{gate})$, where $\mathrm{SiLU}(x) = x \cdot \mathrm{sigmoid}(x)$ (the *sigmoid linear unit*, a.k.a. Swish — hence "Swi"; sigmoid is Chapter 1's 0-to-1 squash) is a smooth ramp:

<SwigluFigure />

One pathway proposes what to write; the other decides, per dimension and per token, how much of it gets through. (The second projection is also why Llama's `intermediate_size` is ~3.5$d$ rather than Chapter 3's 4$d$: SwiGLU needs three matrices where ReLU needed two ($2 \times d \times 4d = 8d^2$ weights before; $3 \times d \times e$ now), so keeping the $8d^2$ budget exactly would shrink the expansion to $e = 8d^2/3d \approx 2.7d$ — Llama rounds up to 3.5$d$, spending a bit extra.) It wins consistently and nobody is quite sure why; the [paper that established it](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05202) ends, verbatim: "We offer no explanation as to why these architectures seem to work; we attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence."

Neither change alters the story of the machine: the field ran a decade-long ablation study, and these won.

## Multimodality: everything becomes a token

Frontier models see images, hear audio, and increasingly emit both — and required almost no new machinery to do it. The recipe is to convert other modalities *into tokens* and reuse the entire apparatus:

1. A **vision encoder** (a [Vision Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11929), ViT) chops the image into a grid of patches (16×16 pixels each in the original), flattens each patch, and treats it exactly like a token embedding. No convolutions; the same blocks as Chapter 3, usually without the causal mask (it's an encoder: the whole image is already on the table, nothing is predicted left-to-right, so no mask is needed — the encoder lineage, mask-free cousin of the decoder stack we've been building).
2. A small **adapter** projects the encoder's output vectors into the language model's embedding space.
3. The LLM proceeds as if the image were a few hundred words it had read. ([CLIP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020), Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining, supplied the standard vision encoder — trained so an image's vector lands near its caption's, leaving the adapter a mere change of coordinates. Audio follows with a speech encoder.)

<VitFigure />

An image is two-dimensional, though, and the flattening step destroys that: patches are laid out in reading order, so the patch directly *above* this one is now just "sixteen positions back," indistinguishable from any other neighbor. How does the model know the grid? The same way the transformer knew word order: it doesn't, until you tell it — attention is a set operation (Chapter 3), so spatial structure arrives as position information. The original ViT did the laziest possible thing: a learned position vector per patch slot, exactly like GPT-2's position table, with no 2D-ness built in — and training *discovered* the geometry anyway: plot which position embeddings resemble which and a clean 2D grid emerges, each patch most similar to its true spatial neighbors (one of the paper's best figures). Modern vision stacks make the structure explicit instead — giving each patch a (row, column) coordinate pair and encoding the two axes separately, e.g. 2D RoPE (recall RoPE rotates independent 2D pairs of dimensions; here some pairs rotate by *row* and others by *column*) — which matters for the same reason RoPE beat the position table in text: formulas generalize to image sizes the table never saw.

Early multimodal systems bolted encoders onto finished LLMs; current frontier models are *natively multimodal* — trained on mixed text/image/audio streams from the start. The transformer was never a language model. It is a sequence model, and prose, code, screenshots, and speech all reach it the same way: as sequences of embeddings.

## Sidebar: the road not (yet) taken

The transformer's quadratic attention keeps inspiring challengers — most prominently **state-space models** like [Mamba](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752), which are, with real irony, a modernized recurrence: constant-size state and linear-time inference — the very design attention replaced in Chapter 3's opening. They now match transformers at moderate scale, and ideas from them show up in production as *hybrids*: a few attention layers among many linear-time layers. The transformer's real moat is nine years of tooling, kernels, and hardware co-evolution — watch the challengers without reorganizing your mental model around them.

Step back and look at what we built. We took Chapter 3's 124M-parameter GPT-2 and, field by field: made its MLPs sparse (520M total, 180M active), shared its KV heads (cache ÷8), swapped its position table for rotations (context extendable), renamed its norms and activations, and taught it to eat pixels. At no point did we touch the skeleton. That is the state of the art: the 2017 architecture, relentlessly cost-optimized.

Something should be nagging you about this chapter, though. We counted every parameter and never once said what any of them *does*. We know the shapes of all the containers and nothing about the contents — half a billion numbers whose dimensions we chose and whose values we can't read. Surely someone has tried to look inside? The next chapter is about the people who did, and what they found there.
